The Design and Technology Teaching Module for Form 1 has been developed to help teachers
implement the 2023 revised Design and Technology syllabus. The subject first appeared in Zambian
Curriculum in 2013 in place of the Industrial Arts subjects – Geometrical and Mechanical Drawing,
Woodwork and Metalwork. The 2013 Design and Technology was integrated with four TEVET
courses i.e. Bricklaying, Carpentry, Electrical and Metal Fabrications. Learners taking Design and
Technology were assessed and certified at Trade Test III Level by TEVETA in any of the trade
courses of the candidate’s choice. However, the 2023 Design and Technology syllabus does not
include the TEVET content but contains the four components: Materials and Manufacturing,
Research and Entrepreneurship, Graphic Communication and Systems Technology. In order to
respond to national policies and aspirations on economic transformation and job creation as well as
human and social development, the Vocation Education Training (VET) syllabuses that were initially
integrated in the Design and Technology syllabus will now be obtained from TEVETA by individual
schools.
The revised 2023 Design and Technology syllabus places more emphasis on STEM education which
refers to the application of scientific and mathematical principles to design and develop technological
solutions.
 Science: Understanding of materials, their properties and how they can be used in designs.
 Technology: Using software, tools and equipment to design, prototype and manufacture
products.
 Engineering: Application of engineering principles when designing and developing
solutions.
 Mathematics: Using mathematical concepts such as geometry, measurement and calculation
to design and optimize solutions
Thus, in learning Design and Technology, STEM will help learners to:
 design and develop innovative products and solutions
 analyse and evaluate the effectiveness of designs
 optimize and improve existing designs
 develop problem-solving skills and critical thinking
In responding to the Curriculum Design of Competence Based, the module is based on the topics,
specific competences and learning activities that have been anchored on the following Key
Competences which learners should demonstrate at Ordinary level Secondary education:
 Analytical Thinking: Process of breaking down complex information into components and
understanding how they are interconnected
 Collaboration: The act of working with others to achieve results as a team.
 Communication: The ability to share ideas, thoughts, information and messages concisely
and precisely
 Creativity and Innovation: The ability to create new ideas and products by applying
processes and introducing new techniques that can add value.

Summary
Joining metals is a fundamental process in metalworking. Temporary joining methods such as bolts,
nuts, and screws allow for parts to be disassembled and reassembled when needed, making them ideal
for maintenance, adjustments, or mobility. In contrast, permanent methods such as welding,
soldering, riveting, and seaming provide strong, long-lasting bonds that ensure durability and
structural integrity, especially in load-bearing or high-stress applications. Understanding the
differences between these methods helps in selecting the most appropriate technique based on the
purpose, material, cost and required strength.

TOPIC: MECHANISMS
SUB-TOPIC: - LEVERS
Introduction
A mechanism is a system of parts (links, gears, levers, etc.) designed to transfer or transform motion
and force to perform a specific task. Mechanisms are fundamental in machines, vehicles, robotics,
and everyday tools. Mechanisms are the building blocks of machines, enabling controlled motion and
force transfer. Understanding them helps in designing efficient machines, from simple tools to
advanced robotics.
Overview
In this sub-topic emphasis will be put on understanding the three classes of levers, and their
application in mechanical systems. A lever is a simple machine that consists of a rigid bar or beam
that pivots around a fixed point, called a fulcrum. Levers are used in mechanisms to amplify forces,
change the direction of forces, or achieve mechanical advantage. When an effort is applied to one
end, it causes a load to be moved at the other end. In a lever mechanism, three aspects are found;
these are an effort (input), a fulcrum (or pivot point) and the Load (output).

friendly designs of structures.
 Problem solving: Learners identify, analyse, and find solutions to mechanical system
failures, and devise new, stronger systems.
Key Terms
 Input: It is the effort applied to a mechanism or a machine to do the work.
 Output: It is the total work done by a mechanism or a machine.
 Mechanism: A mechanism is a device which changes an input motion and force into a desired
output motion and force

Suggested Learning Points
Classification of levers
 Levers are classified into three main classes. Each class has the effort, load and fulcrum
arranged differently. The class of a lever depends on the relative position of the load, effort
and fulcrum.
 The load is the object you are trying to move.
 The effort is the force applied to move the load
 The fulcrum (pivot) is the point around which the load and the effort revolve.
TYPES OF LEVERS
Class 1 levers
 In this type of class, the fulcrum is placed between the effort and the load.
 To increase the mechanical advantage of this class, the fulcrum must be moved closer to the
load. This will result in using less input to move a heavy load.
 Some examples of the first-class lever include a crowbar, claw hammer, see-saw, pliers,
tinsnips, scales, forging tong and screw driver.
Class 2 levers
 Class 2 types of levers have the load between the fulcrum and the effort.
 To obtain the mechanical advantage, the second-class lever, the load must be moved nearer
to the fulcrum. To move a heavy load, the effort will have to move over a much greater
distance than the load.
 Examples of the second-class levers include wheelbarrow, pair of nutcrackers, car bonnet,
crate shifter, bottle opener and brake pedal
Class 3 levers
 In class 3 levers, the effort and the load are on the same side of the fulcrum, with the effort
placed between the load and the fulcrum.
 Unlike in classes 1 and 2 levers, this class does not have any mechanical advantage. It is a
mechanical disadvantage because the input force (effort) is greater than the force produced at
the load.
 The effort moves a smaller distance than it is to the load. Examples of class 3 levers include
a pair of tweezers, a fishing rod, tower crane

Reverse Motion Linkages
 In the Reverse Motion Linkage, the output motion is the reverse of the input motion. The

good number of machines to make them move in opposite directions.
 Reverse motion linkages are used in making hand pumps, borehole mechanisms, and squeeze

Push – Pull Motion Linkages
 These are types of linkages which are used to make movement in the same direction as the
input. The push and pull transmit motion around an obstacle.
 The linkage connects two levers together, ensuring that both move in the same direction at
the same time.
 A good example of a push-and-pull linkage is the wiper mechanism used in cars to clean the
windscreen.
Parallel Motion Linkages
 They are types of linkage systems with levers set at a distance apart and parallel to each other
at all times. Parallel linkages are used to transmit output motion in the same direction as the
input motion over a longer distance. Any movement they make will always be parallel.
Parallel linkages are very useful in things such as tool boxes, lazy tongs, folding burglar door
bars and a cloth drying rack.
 In some cases, Parallel motion linkages, by changing the positions of the fulcrum, can be used
to copy, enlarge or reduce drawings, e.g. in a pantograph. A pantograph is a sketch graph of
parallel linkages, which is usually fixed to the drawing (drawing board) and used to copy,
enlarge and reduce drawings at the same time.

Introduction
A design process is a step by step systematic way of solving a problem. It is a structured approach
to problem-solving that helps learners create functional, innovative, and user-centered solutions. It
explains a series of steps or stages that are to be followed to come up with a product. These steps are
recorded on papers which are later bound (put together) to form a portfolio. When a portfolio is ready,
an artefact or product is then made.
The design process is not always the same. It varies from product to product or with respect to
industry preferences. There are basically three steps involved in the design process; the Process,
Portfolio production and Product making.
 The process –outlines the steps to be followed when designing the artefact.
 Portfolio production –how the pages of the portfolio are expected to be arranged.
 Product making – process of making an artefact/product.

 Divisional Structure: A structure in which departments are organised by product or service,

 Matrix Structure: A structure in which departments are organised by project or team, with
individuals from different departments working together on a project-by-project basis.
Key Considerations in Designing an Organisational Structure
 Business Objectives: The organisational structure should be designed to support the
achievement of the business's objectives.
 Size and Complexity: The organisational structure should be appropriate for the size and
complexity of the business.
 Communication and Decision-Making: The organisational structure should facilitate
effective communication and decision-making within the organisation.

Planning
Definition
 Thinking and working out what to do about something before it happens.
 In your business, planning means thinking about and working out what to do in the future to
improve your business
Planning for a business
Before embarking on a business, think of this;
 What goods or raw materials do I need?
 How much I need?
 Where can I buy the goods and materials?
 How much will these goods or raw materials cost me?
 When do I need the goods or raw materials?
Financial Planning
Definition
 This is a process of predicting revenues and costs and then comparing the estimated profits
with the cost of investment.
 It outlines the financial goals, objectives, and strategies of a business

Design and Technology Studies Teaching Module: Form 1 – Term 3
106
Importance of financial planning
 Helps to achieve financial stability
 Helps individuals to make informed decision
 It reduces financial stress
 Helps to achieve financial goals
Financial planning tips
 Assess your current financial situation – consider your income and expenses
 Create a budget – outline what you need
 Prioritise your budget– arrange your budget items in order of priority
 Invest your resources – think of investing your resources for future use
Parts of financial planning
Financial Planning consist of:
1. Budgeting
 A plan for how the business will allocate its resources and spend its money, including
operating budgets, capital budgets, and cash budgets.
 Making a budget helps a business allocate resources, evaluate performance, and formulate
plans.
2. Costing a product
 Coming up with all the costs associated with producing a product
 It is calculated by finding the sum of direct and indirect costs of producing the product or
service. It is summarised as;
 Direct labour cost + Direct material costs + Indirect costs =Total production cost
3. Sales and cost forecast
 A plan which shows the expected sales against the total production cost.
 It helps to determine the expected profit or loss for a business
 It can be summarised as; Sales – costs = Profit/loss
4. Cash flow plan
 This is a forecast which shows how much cash you expect to come (flow) into and go (flow)
out of your business each month.
 It is the difference between receipts from the sales and the amount spent on expenses such as
raw material, interest paid on loans, dividends paid to shareholders etc.
 Money received into the business adds to its capital reserves while money paid out reduces
them.

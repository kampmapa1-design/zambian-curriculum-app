The Design and Technology Teaching Module for Form 1 has been developed to help teachers
implement the 2023 revised Design and Technology syllabus. The subject first appeared in Zambian
Curriculum in 2013 in place of the Industrial Arts subjects – Geometrical and Mechanical Drawing,
Woodwork and Metalwork. The 2013 Design and Technology syllabus was integrated with
four TEVET courses i.e. Bricklaying, Carpentry, Electrical and Metal Fabrications. Learners
taking Design and Technology were assessed and certified at Trade Test III Level by TEVETA in any
of the trade courses of the candidate’s choice. However, the 2023 Design and Technology syllabus
does not include the TEVET content but contains the four components: Materials and
Manufacturing, Research and Entrepreneurship, Graphic Communication and Systems
Technology. In order to respond to national policies and aspirations on economic transformation
and job creation as well as human and social development, the Vocation Education Training (VET)
syllabuses that were initially integrated in the Design and Technology syllabus will now be
obtained from TEVETA by individual schools.
The revised 2023 Design and Technology syllabus places more emphasis on STEM education which
refers to the application of scientific and mathematical principles to design and
develop technological solutions.
 Science: Understanding of materials, their properties and how they can be used in designs.
 Technology: Using software, tools and equipment to design, prototype and
manufacture products.
 Engineering: Application of engineering principles when designing and developing solutions.
 Mathematics: Using mathematical concepts such asgeometry, measurement
and calculation to design and optimise solutions
Thus, in learning Design and Technology, STEM will help learners to:
 design and develop innovative products and solutions
 analyse and evaluate the effectiveness of designs
 optimize and improve existing designs
 develop problem-solving skills and critical thinking
In responding to the Curriculum Design of Competence Based, the module is based on the topics,
specific competences and learning activities that have been anchored on the following Key

Suggested learning points
Properties of Metals
Mechanical Properties:
These include: Strength, hardness, toughness, elasticity, plasticity, brittleness, and ductility and
malleability. They show how metals behave under load.
 Strength: This is the property that enables a metal to resist deformation under load.
- Compressive strength is the ability of a material to withstand compression force.
- Tensile strength is the ability of a material to resist being pulled apart by opposing forces.
- Shear strength is the ability of a metal to resist being fractured by opposing forces not
acting in a straight line.
- Fatigue strength is the maximum load a material can withstand without failure during a
large number of reversals of load.

7
- Impact strength is the ability of a metal to resist suddenly applied loads and is measured
in foot-pounds of force
- Bending strength is the ability of a metal to bend without deforming or breaking.
- Torsion strength is the ability of a metal torsional forces
 Hardness: - is the ability to resist scratching, abrasion, or cutting
 Toughness: - is the property that enables a material to withstand shock and be deformed
without rupturing.
 Elasticity: - is the ability of a material to return to its original shape after the load is removed.
 Plasticity: - is the ability of materials to undergo irreversible deformation without fracture
or damage.
 Ductility: - is the ability of a metal to be stretched or drawn into a thin wire without breaking.
 Malleability is the ability of a metal to be hammed or rolled into thin sheets without breaking
or cracking.
 Brittleness: - is the ability of a metal to break or shatter before it deforms if bent or struck
with a sharp blow.
Chemical Properties:
 Oxidation and Corrosion: Most metals can undergo oxidation and corrosion, reacting with
oxygen and moisture to form rust or oxides and other compounds.
 Corrosion Resistance: The property that enables a material to resist entering into chemical
combination with other substancesfrom attacks by atmospheric, chemical, or
electrochemical conditions.
Ecological Properties:
Recyclability: -Most metals are highly recyclable, meaning they can be melted down and reused
without significant loss of quality. This characteristic contributes to the sustainability and
conservation of resources. On the other hand, some metals such as lead or mercury are toxic and
they are a danger to human beings and for the environment and hence require careful disposal.

Suggested Learning Points
Classification of metals
There are numerous types of metals, each with its unique properties and applications. Here are
some different types of metals:
 Ferrous Metals
These are metals that contain iron as the primary element. Examples include iron, steel, and
stainless steel. Ferrous metals are known for their strength and durability.
 Non-Ferrous Metals
These are metals that do not contain iron as the primary element. Examples include
aluminum, copper, zinc, lead, and tin. Non-ferrous metals are valued for their light weight,
corrosion resistance, and electrical conductivity.
 Precious Metals
These are rare and highly valuable metals. Examples include gold, silver, platinum, and
palladium. Precious metals are sought after for their beauty, rarity, and use in jewelry,
currency, and investment.
 Base Metals
These are common metals that are not considered precious. Examples include copper,
aluminum, zinc, and nickel. Base metals are widely used in various industries such as
construction, manufacturing, and electronics.
 Alloyed Metals

Iron Production
Iron is a basic element of the earth’s covering. It is the second most abundant metal in the Earth’s
crust. It is found combined with other elements in the form of Iron ore. The three main ores are:
Hematite, Magnetite, and Limonite. At the iron works, the ore is mixed with coke and limestone.
The coke serves as fuel and a reducing agent while limestone acts as a flux, for the removal of
impurities.
 Mining: Iron ore is extracted from the earth through open- pit or
underground mining.
 Crushing and Screening: The iron ore is crushed and
screened to separate the ore into different grades.
 Smelting: The iron ore is smelted in a blast furnace to
produce pig iron.
 Refining: The pig iron is refined in a steelmaking furnace to
produce steel.

transportation, and consumer goods.
 Machinery: Iron is used in the manufacture of machinery, including engines, gears, and

 Electrical Applications: Iron is used in electrical applications, including transformers, motors,

Steel Manufacturing Processes
The steel manufacturing process can be divided into six steps: Making the iron, primary steelmaking,
secondary steelmaking, casting, primary forming, and secondary forming.
Step 1: Making the Iron
Steel is a metal alloy made of iron and carbon. To do this, limestone, coke, and iron ore are combined
and charged into a blast furnace. The elements are melted together to create a hot metal known as
molten iron.
Step 2: Primary Steelmaking
The molten metal is mixed from step 1 is mixed with scrap steel in the Basic Oxygen Furnace. Then,
oxygen is forced through the furnace to remove the impurities in the molten iron. With an electric
arc furnace, as the name suggests, electricity is forced through the furnace to purify the iron. The
process results in raw steel.
Step 3: Secondary Steelmaking
There are different grades of steel. The grades are determined by the carbon content and elements
that are added to make a specific steel. This is done with different techniques such as stirring and
ladle injections.
Step 4: Casting
During this process, molten iron is cast into moulds for cooling hence, setting the shape of the steel.
This causes a thin hard shell to form. The material formed is malleable and can be worked into the
desired shapes of flat sheets, beams, wires, or thin strips.
Step 5: Primary Forming
This stage involves fine-tuning the moulded steel into desired shapes and surface finish using a hot
roller. Some examples include bloom, billet, and slab.
Step 6: Secondary Forming
This stage creates the final shape and properties of the steel. It is accomplished with different
methods such as shaping, machining, joining, coating, heat treatment, and surface treatment. At
this stage, the steel is fully shaped, formed, and ready for use and processing in various applications.

Methods used to manufacture plastic products
Plastic’s raw materials (that are in powder, sheet, viscous fluids, pellets and granules forms) are
converted into plastic products using one of the following methods;
1. Bending
 This is a deforming method used in plastics sheets up to 6mm.
 This applies to Thermoplastics which cannot be bent when cold unless when subjected to
heat and they then acquire an elastic form.
 Heat is applied along a bend line in order to bend the materials with small curve/radius. They
are bent using a strip heater or line bender which will heat the plastic in a straight line, so it
can be bent by hand.
2. Press forming
 This is a process of forming plastics by means of double-curved bending.
 Two matching formers are used.
 The plastics is heated in an oven and then clamped between matching formers, referred to
as the male former and female former.

SUB-TOPIC: – PLANE GEOMETRY
Introduction
Plane geometry is a study of shapes, sizes and positions of objects on a flat surface. It is the
foundation for all drawings. Ideally, all plane figures begin with a curved or straight line. With lines,
various objects can be designed, constructed and presented. When two lines meet at one point,
they form an angle. Equally when three lines are joined end to end, they result in a drawing known
as a triangle. Conversely, four lines joined end to end form a drawing called a quadrilateral.
Let us now learn how each of these drawings can be constructed on a paper.

Construction of quadrilaterals

- Draw one side BA
- Construct the right angle at B
- With centre B and radius of the side, draw an arc to cut the perpendicular at C
- With centres A and C in turn and radius of the side, draw arcs to intersect at D
- Join CD and DA to complete the square
(ii) A rectangle given the length of diagonals and one side
- Draw the diagonal BD and construct a circle on it
- With centre B and a radius of the given side, draw an arc to cut circumference at C
- With centre D and a radius of the given side, draw an arc to cut circumference at A
- Join BC, CD, DA and AB to complete the rectangle
(iii) A rhombus given the length of diagonal and side
- Draw the diagonal AC
- With centres A and C in turn and radius of side draw arcs above the line to intersect at

- With centres A and C in turn and radius of side draw arcs below the line to intersect at
D
- Join AB, BC, CD and DA to complete the rhombus

- Smooth and often rhythmic.
- Common in timekeeping and ergonomic designs.

Suggested Learning Points
Costing and pricing
 Costing - The process of calculating how much you have spent to produce a product or offer
a service
 Pricing – The process of arriving at a figure after adding a profit margin to the production
cost.
Product Costing and Pricing
 Product costing and pricing are crucial elements of a marketing plan.
 They determine the financial viability of a product or service.
 When you add the total production cost, you are costing your product.
 When you add the production cost and profit margin, you are pricing your product.
Concepts in Product Costing
 Fixed Costs: Costs that remain the same even if the quantity of the product changes, such
as rent, salaries, and equipment.
 Variable Costs: Costs that vary with the quantity of the product, such as raw materials,
labour, and packaging.
 Total Cost: The sum of fixed and variable costs.
Pricing Strategies
 Cost-Plus Pricing: A pricing strategy that adds a mark-up to the total cost of the product.
 Value-Based Pricing: A pricing strategy that sets the price based on the perceived value of
the product to the customer.
 Competitive Pricing: A pricing strategy that sets the price based on the prices of similar
products in the market.
 Penetration Pricing: A pricing strategy that sets a low initial price to attract customers and
gain market share.
Considerations in Pricing
 Target Market: The price must be acceptable to the target market.
 Competition: The price must be competitive with similar products in the market.
 Product Life Cycle: The price may need to be adjusted at different stages of the product life
cycle.
 Profit Margin: The price must ensure a sufficient profit margin for the business.
Assessment Guideline
Formative

ability to accurately determine costs and set prices.

APPENDIX (I)
Task:
a) Relevance: the task should be in tandem with the specific competence
b) Authenticity: It should reflect a real-life situation that will require application of knowledge
c) Complexity: It should require learners to demonstrate higher-order thinking and problem-
solving.
d) Depth: It should provoke learners to explore the task in-depth and evoke their attitude or
demonstrate mastery of knowledge or skill.
EXAMPLE OF A TASK:
DESIGN AND CONSTRUCT A PENCIL RACK
You have a lot of pens, pencils, crayons and markers in class while having Graphic Communication
lessons and at times they drop on the floor; sometimes making it difficult to use them. Using
available tools, design and make an item, that you will use to keep pens, pencils and markers
preventing them from falling to the floor.
Question:
1. Design and make an item to keep pencils, pens and other items.
a) Factors to consider - joint strength, durability, and aesthetic appeal
b) Suitability of the material
c) Hand tool skills: Demonstrate proficiency in using hand tools, including:
d) Precision and accuracy: Achieve precise and accurate joints, demonstrating attention
to detail and quality craftsmanship.
2. Drawing: design and construction process, including measurements, and notes on tool usage
and techniques.
This task meets the criteria:
a) Relevance: Construction industry and traditional craftsmanship.
b) Authenticity: Real-life scenario, requiring learners to apply skills and knowledge in a
practical
c) Complexity: involves design, material selection, hand tool skills and joint
construction.
d) Depth: requires learners to demonstrate skills, precision, accuracy attention to detail
and knowledge
Assessment Criteria:
1. Design (20%): Effectiveness of design, consideration of factors and quality of planning

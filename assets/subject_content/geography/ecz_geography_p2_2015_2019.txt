Model answer: Two well-known Zambian irrigation schemes are the Mpongwe Wheat Scheme (wheat and maize) and the Gwembe Valley Development Scheme (cotton and wheat, drawing on the Zambezi/Lake Kariba). ECZ Geography Paper 2 (2218/2), 2015, Question 2: (a) What are deciduous trees, and what are some examples found in Zambia? (b) Discuss the significance of forests to human beings. (c) What is the relevance of the Forestry Department in managing indigenous forests? Model answer: Deciduous trees are trees that shed their leaves periodically (typically in the dry season); examples found in Zambia include mukwa, mululu, mubanga and the baobab. Forests matter to people in many ways: they supply building materials (poles, thatching grass), raw material for the curio trade (carvings, fibre, baskets), fuel (firewood and charcoal), food for people and animals (fruit, browse), and industrial raw material (timber for sawmilling, boat-building, pulp and paper). Ecologically they help purify the air, shelter wildlife, act as windbreaks, and protect soil from erosion and drying out, which in turn supports farming; timber is also used for rail sleepers, furniture and utility poles. Zambia's Forestry Department manages indigenous forests by running reafforestation and afforestation programmes where trees have been cleared, educating communities on the value of forests, employing guards and rangers to protect them, establishing forest reserves as wildlife sanctuaries, discouraging harmful practices like shifting cultivation, protecting water catchment areas, and promoting sustainable use so future generations continue to benefit.

Model answer: Zambian copper output fell through much of the 1990s and only began rising steadily again after 2000, with the state-owned ZCCM (and its successor KCM) historically accounting for a much larger share of production than the newer, smaller mining companies -- though even after 2000 ZCCM/KCM's own output stayed uneven. Model answer: Road and water transport open up remote and rural areas, create employment (drivers, mechanics, engineers), make it possible to exploit natural resources and move raw materials, boost trade between countries, support industrialisation, and make it easier for governments to deliver services and administer their territory. ECZ Geography Paper 2 (2218/2), 2015, Question 8: With reference to hydro-electric power in the Southern African sub-region: (a) name two hydro-electric power stations; (b) explain the factors that favoured their construction; (c) explain the positive and negative effects of dam construction, and why the sub-region still has insufficient hydroelectricity despite its large potential. Model answer: Major hydro-electric power stations in the sub-region include Kariba (on the Zambezi, shared by Zambia and Zimbabwe), Cahora Bassa (Mozambique) and Inga (on the Congo River, DRC). Such stations are typically sited where there is a large, dependable volume of water, natural waterfalls, rapids or narrow gorges that create the head of pressure needed to drive turbines, firm bedrock to found the dam wall on, and where the necessary capital, technical skill and machinery are available; low local population also eases construction. Model answer: SADC's energy policy objectives include developing regional electrification (to support transport and agriculture) by exploiting the region's substantial hydro-electric potential, and promoting interconnection of national power grids so that generation and distribution capacity is used more efficiently across member states. Model answer: Zambia recognises several land tenure systems: customary (traditional) land, held in trust by chiefs on behalf of their communities, who may use it but not sell it; state land, acquired and controlled by the President in the public interest and administered through the Ministry of Lands; and leasehold land, granted for a fixed term (up to 99 years) with presidential consent and a certificate of title. Model answer: Doubling time is the number of years it would take a population to double in size at its current growth rate. A young population is one with a high proportion of children and young adults (and a low median age), giving it strong future growth potential. Model answer: The Central Business District (CBD) is the commercial core of a town or city, where shops, offices and businesses cluster and land values and rents are highest, falling with distance from the centre. Peak hours are the times of day when urban traffic congestion is heaviest -- typically the morning and late-afternoon commute, and around midday as people go to and from lunch. Urban blight in this context refers to the pull that urban services and amenities (like electricity) exert on rural people, drawing them to migrate to towns in the hope of a better standard of living. ECZ Geography Paper 2 (2218/2), 2017, Question 10: (a) What are squatter settlements and shanty compounds? (b) Describe the features that distinguish housing in low-density areas from shanty compounds. Model answer: A squatter settlement is a residential area that has developed without legal title to the land or official planning permission. Shanty compounds are similarly unplanned settlements, typically lacking reliable access to electricity, piped water and proper sewerage. Housing in low-density areas is generally built of concrete block with proper roofing (asbestos or iron sheet), legally connected to electricity and piped water, served by planned road networks and sewer systems, and often protected by walls, fencing or security personnel, with larger plots, fewer occupants per house, and well-maintained surroundings. Shanty-compound housing, by contrast, is often built of cheaper or improvised materials (brick, tin, cardboard, mud), may rely on illegal electricity connections, shallow wells and pit latrines, is more densely occupied and unplanned in layout, and generally lacks the security and maintained infrastructure found in low-density areas.

ECZ Geography Paper 2 (2218/2), 2018, Question 3: With reference to the traditional land tenure system in Zambia: (a) explain its main features and its weaknesses for improving crop farming; (b) give examples of farming practices under this system and explain how they contribute to soil improvement. Model answer: Under Zambia's traditional (customary) land tenure system, land is held communally, with no individual title; it cannot be sold, only allocated by traditional leaders such as chiefs and headmen, who may apportion parts of it to families within the community. This system has real weaknesses for improving crop farming: land held this way cannot be used as loan collateral, tends to be under-utilised since occupants do not own it outright, attracts little permanent investment or development, and is prone to disputes over who has rights to it. Farming practices under this system include the Chitemene system practised in Northern, Luapula, Central and North-Western Provinces, in which ash from burned branches fertilises the soil and residual charcoal continues to add nutrients over time; crop rotation, which restores soil nitrogen; bush fallowing under shifting cultivation, which lets soil nutrients recover; and, among the Lozi, the use of cattle manure to enrich fields.

Model answer: A head of water is the height from which water falls or flows swiftly -- typically a natural waterfall or steep drop -- that hydro-electric stations use to drive their turbines. Zambian HEP stations built at such natural falls include Victoria Falls, Mambilima Falls, Musonda Falls, Lunzua Falls and Chishimba Falls. Rural electrification matters because it reduces reliance on wood fuel (helping conserve forests and reduce smoke emissions linked to climate change), gives rural households a cleaner and cheaper source of power for lighting and appliances, benefits schools and clinics, attracts agro-based industry and the jobs that come with it, improves communication (e.g. powering mobile network infrastructure), and can reduce rural-to-urban migration. Hydro-electric power is clean, creates reservoirs useful for fishing, irrigation, tourism and transport, is relatively simple to operate once built, and can transmit power over long distances -- but it is expensive to build, its output fluctuates with rainfall and can drop sharply in drought, its dams are vulnerable to silting from soil erosion, and the electricity it generates cannot be stored. Diesel generators, by contrast, are unaffected by drought, are relatively cheap to buy and install, and can be sited anywhere power is needed -- but they pollute the environment, are costly to run and maintain, and depend on a non-renewable fuel source.

Model answer: Road transport offers door-to-door delivery, flexibility to travel where there is no formal road, the ability to reach even remote areas, and freedom for vehicle owners to travel when and how they choose -- and it is generally faster than rail. ECZ Geography Paper 2 (2218/2), 2019, Question 4: (a) Name crops grown by subsistence farmers in Western Province, and explain how the physical environment shapes farming methods used across the province. ECZ Geography Paper 2 (2218/2), 2019, Question 6: Regarding petroleum refining and transport in Angola: (a) why was pipeline chosen to move petroleum to other countries in the sub-region; (b) explain how petroleum is refined and its major products; (c) what are the main uses of refined petroleum products? Model answer: Pipelines were chosen to move Angolan petroleum because they are among the cheapest ways to move oil over land (relying mainly on gravity and a few pumping stations), are relatively safe since the pipes are buried underground, can guarantee a steady supply to refineries and consumers, are relatively easy to repair, and -- although expensive to build -- are cheap to run. Petroleum is refined by fractional distillation: crude oil is heated to high temperatures in a distillation tower, and its various hydrocarbon fractions boil off and rise up the tower, condensing at different heights according to their boiling points (lighter fractions like petrol condensing near the top, heavier fractions like bitumen near the bottom). Climate matters too: heavy year-round equatorial rainfall makes roads and railways expensive to build and maintain, while drier savanna areas are cheaper to build in, though muddy or earth roads there can still delay traffic in the wet season; rivers near the equator flow reliably, while savanna rivers may dry up in the dry season and only become navigable when summer rains raise their level, and heavy rains can also wash away bridges.

The Biology Form 1 Teaching Module has been developed to guide teachers on how to facilitate
competence-based lessons at this level. The module aims to deliver quality education that is aligned
with the Competence-Based Curriculum which puts emphasis on the acquisition of the 21st Century
Competences by developing requisite knowledge, skills, values and positive attitudes. These
competences will enable learners to live and grow into productive and useful members of their
communities and the Zambian society. The Teaching Module offers a guide to teachers as to how they
will facilitate the learning in their biology classes through well-structured learners’activities and
pedagogies. Therefore, teachers are encouraged to research widely and not only rely on the module.
At no time is the whole class approach encouraged but rather, the teacher is encouraged to facilitate
learning in small cooperative groups. It is envisaged that by emphasising cooperative learning,
teachers will create a more engaging and effective learning environment that will foster not only
academic achievement but also essential life skills that will benefit the learners throughout their lives.
This approach aligns with the principles of constructivist learning, where learners actively construct
their own knowledge through social interaction and collaborative problem-solving.
In a case where a learner fails to break through, individualised learning or scaffolding should be
applied so that no one is left behind.

PURPOSE OF THE MODULE
The purpose of the module is to illustrate teaching and learning activities that:
a) are consistent with teaching and learning in a competence-based curriculum,
b) show processes that allow achievement of 21st Century Skills such as creativity, collaboration,
communication and critical thinking,
c) demonstrate how biology can be actively taught with the teacher acting as a facilitator of the
learning process,
d) show what activity-based and learner-centred teaching and learning could look like where
learners actively learn in small cooperative groups.
HOW TO USE THE MODULE
This module provides a collection of suggested learning activities and teaching strategies designed to
enhance learners' scientific competences. These activities emphasise hands-on experiences, such as
manipulating real objects, interacting with nature, and learning through practice. It is crucial to
understand that the desired competences and 21st-century skills will not be achieved solely by
covering Sub-topics but are developed through the way teaching and learning activities are carried
out. Learners are expected to acquire general competences as they interact with the material under
the guidance of the teacher. These essential competences include analytical thinking, effective
communication, collaboration, critical thinking, environmental sustainability, real-world application,
problem-solving, and the development of essential scientific skills.
The activities within this module are intended to be integrated into the teacher's existing lesson plans,
not to replace them entirely. Teachers are encouraged to adapt these suggested activities to suit their
specific class situation, learner abilities, and available resources. The Sub-topics and activities
presented in this module align with the 2024 Biology Syllabus.
Each Sub-topic in the module begins with a "Hook" – an opening statement designed to stimulate
learners' curiosity and motivate them to learn. This could be a provocative question, a relevant
scenario, or something that encourages learners to think critically. Teachers are encouraged to create
their own hooks that best provoke their learners' curiosity and motivation. Consider incorporating
problem-solving scenarios into your hooks. In this Module, you will also find the “Content Tip”,
which is intended to be for the attention of the teacher and NOT the learner, to provide guidance on
the learning activities.

CONCEPT 5.0: COORDINATION & RESPONSE
Introduction
Overview: Coordination and response in living organisms involve specific mechanisms for detecting
and reacting to environmental stimuli. Two key types of responses are tropic responses in plants and
taxic responses in motile organisms, which are critical for survival, growth and reproduction.
In this concept, the sub-topics coordination and response in plants as well as coordination and
response in animals will be covered.
General Competencies: Collaboration, communication, analytical thinking and critical thinking.
Key concepts/words/vocabulary
 Stimulus – A change in the environment of an organism that triggers a response.
 Tropism- A growth response in plants to an external stimulus.
 Phototropism – Growth response of plant parts to light
 Geotropism – Growth response of plant parts to gravity
 Chemotropism – Growth response of plant parts to chemicals.
 Hydrotropism – Growth response of plant parts to water.
 Taxic response – Directional movement response by an organism to an external stimulus.
 Phototaxis – Directional movement by an organism in response to light.
 Chemo taxis – Directional movement by an organism in response to chemicals.
 Geotaxis - Directional movement by an organism in response to gravity.
Sub-topic 5.1- Coordination and response in plants
Introduction
Overview: Imagine you’re walking through a forest and notice that some plants are thriving in the
shade, while others seem to dominate open, sunny areas. At the same time, you see a vine curling
around a tree as though it ‘knows’ where to climb. How do plants, which lack a brain or nerves, 'sense'
their environment and respond so precisely to ensure their survival?
Plants, though lacking a nervous system, exhibit remarkable coordination and response mechanisms
to adapt to their environment. These processes are vital for survival, growth, reproduction, and
ecological balance. A knowledge of coordination and responses in plants can be applied to climate
change mitigation, agriculture and food security, environmental interactions, ecosystem services and
many more.
In this sub-topic, learners will describe tropisms in plants; investigate the effect of light and gravity
on shoots and roots; investigate the effect of water and chemicals on roots; and evaluate the
importance of tropisms.
Specific Competence: Explore tropisms in plants and their relevance

5.1.3: Investigating the effect of water on roots (hydrotropism)

Germinated seeds (e.g., beans or peas), petri dishes, damp cotton wool, dry cotton wool, a divider

Activity 1: Thirsty Roots Experiment
Procedure
i. Put learners into groups of 4-5
ii. Provide each group with the necessary materials.
iii. Ask learners to place damp cotton wool on one side of a petri dish and dry cotton wool on the

iv. Ask learners to use a divider to prevent the damp cotton wool from soaking the dry cotton

vii. Ask learners to cover the petri dish with a lid or plastic wrap to maintain humidity and prevent
the cotton wool from drying out too quickly.
viii. Ask learners to place the dish in a dark environment to eliminate the influence of light. Leave
the setup undisturbed for 3–5 days.
ix. Ask learners to observe and record the direction of the root growth.
x. Ask learners to measure the length of root growth relative to the position of the cotton wools.
xi. Ask learners to make presentations of their findings
Discussion
Consolidate after all the groups have made presentations of their findings.

Consolidate after all the groups have made presentations of their findings.

Sub-topic 5.2. Coordination and response in animal
Introduction
Overview: Coordination and responses in animals are vital for survival and adaptation to dynamic
environments. Taxic responses are critical as they enable animals to move directionally towards or
away from a specific stimulus. The study of coordination and responses in animals particularly taxic
responses provides valuable insights into animal behaviour, survival strategies and ecological roles.
These responses have practical applications in fields like medicine, agriculture, environmental
conservation and pest control management, directly impacting human life and ecosystem
sustainability.
In this sub-topic, learners will explore taxic responses in invertebrates and their relevance. This will
be achieved through describing taxic responses; investigating taxic responses to light, chemicals and
gravity; evaluating the relevance of taxic responses to the organism; applying the mechanism of taxic
responses to eliminate pests and insects in homes and differentiating tropic responses from taxic
responses.
Specific competence:
Explore taxic responses in invertebrates and their relevance

Sub-topic 6.2. Biotic and abiotic interactions
Introduction
Overview: Biotic factors refer to the living components of an ecosystem and the interactions among
them. These include predator-prey relationships, parasite-host dynamics and competition between
individuals for limited resources such as food, mates and nesting sites. Such interactions play a
significant role in determining the distribution and abundance of organisms within an ecosystem.
Abiotic factors on the other hand, are the non-living components of the environment. These include
physical and chemical elements such as temperature, light, water, soil composition, pH and
atmospheric gases. Together, biotic and abiotic factors shape ecosystems and influence how
organisms survive, grow and reproduce.

Sub-topic 6.4 The soil - Composition and Fertility
Introduction
Overview: Soil is a complex mixture of minerals, organic matter, water, air and living organisms.
Soil fertility refers to the soil's ability to provide essential nutrients to plants in adequate amounts and
proportions for optimal growth. Furthermore, soil composition and fertility are essential for
optimising agricultural productivity, ensuring environmental sustainability, supporting ecosystem
health, as well as achieving economic and food security benefits.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 30: (a) Explain how leaves are adapted to carry out photosynthesis. (b) How does a plant obtain and use nitrogen and magnesium? (c) Explain how enzyme activity is affected by pH and substrate concentration. Model answer: Leaves are well adapted for photosynthesis: a thin lamina lets light penetrate easily, a large surface area captures as much light as possible, veins (vascular bundles) supply water via the xylem and carry away sugars via the phloem, stomata allow carbon dioxide in and oxygen out, and chloroplasts (concentrated most heavily in the palisade layer, then the spongy mesophyll) absorb light energy. Plants take up nitrogen from the soil as nitrate or ammonium ions, and magnesium as magnesium ions, both absorbed through the root hairs by active transport; nitrogen is then used mainly to build proteins and amino acids, while magnesium is a key component of chlorophyll. Enzyme activity is highly sensitive to pH: away from an enzyme's optimum pH, its structure (and so its active site) becomes distorted -- denatured -- so the substrate can no longer bind and form an enzyme-substrate complex, and activity falls. Substrate concentration affects reaction rate differently: increasing it speeds up the reaction roughly in proportion, up to the point where all the enzyme's active sites are occupied, after which further increases in substrate have no additional effect.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 31: (a) Compare and contrast gaseous exchange in a fish and an insect. (b) Describe the composition and functions of lymph. Model answer: Insects exchange gases through a tracheal system: air enters through spiracles into the trachea, which branches into fine tracheoles where gas exchange occurs directly with the tissues, aided by movements of the abdomen. Fish, by contrast, exchange gases across the gills: water taken in through the mouth passes over the gill filaments (each covered in thin, blood-rich lamellae) and out through the operculum, with oxygen and carbon dioxide diffusing across the gill surface. The lymphatic system consists of lymphatic capillaries that join into larger vessels, which eventually drain into the bloodstream near the vena cava; lymph is moved along by the contraction of surrounding muscles, with valves preventing backflow. Lymph carries lymphocytes -- produced in the lymph nodes -- which defend the body by producing antibodies and antitoxins, while the fibrous network within lymph nodes traps bacteria for the lymphocytes to destroy.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 32: (a) Explain the factors which reduce immunity to pathogens, and why immunisation against diseases like measles and TB is especially important in children under five. (b) Describe how bilharzia (schistosomiasis) is transmitted, and how it can be prevented and controlled. Model answer: Immunity can be weakened by poor nutrition, the emergence of drug-resistant pathogen strains, repeated exposure to infection, immunosuppressive drugs, and infections such as HIV that directly attack the immune system's lymphocytes. Immunisation matters most for young children because it protects them from serious infections before they naturally encounter them in the community, using the body's own immune response to build lasting resistance -- and because young children are especially vulnerable to complications from diseases like measles and TB, the protection immunisation gives far outweighs its very small risks. Bilharzia is caused by a parasitic flatworm (Schistosoma); infected people release the parasite's eggs into water in urine or faeces, the eggs hatch into larvae that infect freshwater snails, and a further larval stage released from the snail can penetrate human skin during contact with contaminated water, completing the cycle. It can be prevented and controlled by avoiding contact with water known to harbour the parasite, controlling snail populations, draining stagnant water, ensuring safe (boiled or treated) drinking water, and avoiding urinating or defecating near water sources.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 33: (a) Explain the term immunity to disease. (b) Distinguish between active and passive immunity, with an example of each. (c) Discuss how immunity to disease is reduced. Model answer: Immunity is the body's ability to resist infection, based on the presence of antibodies that recognise and neutralise specific pathogens. Active immunity arises when the body itself is stimulated to produce antibodies -- either through natural infection (for example, becoming permanently immune to measles after having had it) or through vaccination with weakened or dead pathogens (for example, BCG vaccination against tuberculosis). Passive immunity, by contrast, occurs when ready-made antibodies are introduced from an external source, giving immediate but temporary protection -- for example, antibodies passed from mother to baby via breast milk or across the placenta, or an anti-tetanus or anti-venom injection. Immunity can be reduced by poor diet (particularly deficiencies in key vitamins and minerals), the evolution of resistant pathogen strains, repeated infection, immunosuppressive drugs, and diseases such as HIV that directly damage the immune system.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 34: (a) Compare and contrast nutrition in a guava plant and a Rhizopus fungus. (b) Discuss the importance of saprophytic nutrition to the environment. (c) Describe the digestion of food in the mouth. Model answer: A guava plant feeds by autotrophic (photosynthetic) nutrition: using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll, it manufactures glucose from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen as a by-product. Rhizopus, a mould, instead feeds saprophytically: it secretes digestive enzymes from its hyphae onto dead organic matter, breaking it down externally before absorbing the soluble products. Saprophytic organisms matter ecologically because they decompose dead material -- preventing its indefinite accumulation -- and recycle nutrients such as carbon and nitrogen back into ecosystems. In the mouth, food is first broken into smaller pieces by chewing (mastication); saliva from the salivary glands moistens and lubricates it for easier swallowing, adjusts its temperature towards one favourable for enzyme action, and provides a mildly alkaline environment in which the enzyme salivary amylase can begin breaking down cooked starch into maltose.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 35: (a) Explain what is meant by aerobic respiration. (b) Describe the adverse health effects of named air pollutants. (c) Explain how parts of the respiratory system are adapted to minimise the entry of air pollutants. Model answer: Aerobic respiration is the release of a relatively large amount of energy from food in living cells using oxygen, producing carbon dioxide and water as by-products. Among air pollutants, carbon monoxide is particularly dangerous: once inhaled, it binds irreversibly to haemoglobin (forming carboxyhaemoglobin), reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity and potentially causing breathlessness, headache or, in severe cases, death; babies of mothers repeatedly exposed to it can be born underweight. Sulphur dioxide irritates the nose, throat and airways, causing coughing, wheezing and chest tightness. The respiratory tract defends against such pollutants using cilia -- tiny hair-like projections lining the airways -- which sweep a protective layer of mucus (trapping dust and pathogens) up and away from the lungs, while macrophages on the alveolar surface engulf and destroy any particles or pathogens that do get through.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 36: (a) Describe the life cycle of a mosquito. (b) With reference to bilharzia, discuss its signs/symptoms and method of transmission. Model answer: A mosquito undergoes complete metamorphosis in four stages: females lay eggs in water, which hatch into aquatic larvae that feed on plankton; larvae develop into a relatively inactive pupal stage undergoing internal transformation, from which the adult mosquito eventually emerges. Bilharzia's signs and symptoms include blood in the urine, lower abdominal pain, coughing, fever, an enlarged spleen and liver, and skin itching. It is transmitted when infected people release parasite eggs into water; these hatch into larvae that infect freshwater snails, which in turn release a further larval stage capable of penetrating human skin on contact with contaminated water.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 37: (a) Describe anaerobic respiration in yeast. (b) Explain the use of yeast in brewing and baking. (c) Compare and contrast anaerobic respiration in man and in yeast. Model answer: Yeast respires anaerobically through alcoholic fermentation: its enzyme zymase breaks glucose down into carbon dioxide and ethanol, releasing a relatively small amount of energy compared with aerobic respiration (glucose --zymase--> carbon dioxide + alcohol + energy). In brewing, yeast is added to a sugary mixture, and the zymase-driven fermentation produces alcohol, which is then concentrated by distillation; in baking, yeast added to dough ferments sugars in the flour mixture, and the carbon dioxide produced causes the dough to rise. Human anaerobic respiration differs from yeast's in its products and mechanism: human muscle cells produce lactic acid (no gas is released) using different enzymes, whereas yeast produces both alcohol and carbon dioxide gas via zymase.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 38: (a) Explain the term respiration. (b) Distinguish gaseous exchange in fish and insects. (c) Explain how the alveolus is adapted for gaseous exchange. Model answer: Respiration is the release of energy from food in living cells; it includes internal respiration (energy released inside cells, whether aerobically or anaerobically) and external respiration (the physical exchange of gases with the environment, such as breathing). Fish exchange gases across gill lamellae as water flows over them from mouth to operculum, while insects exchange gases through a tracheal system of air tubes opening at spiracles. The alveolus is well suited to gas exchange because it has a large surface area, a moist lining (gases must dissolve before diffusing), a thin wall (minimising diffusion distance), a rich blood supply maintaining a steep diffusion gradient, and good ventilation that continually refreshes the air within it.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 39: (a) What are the characteristics of the alveolus as a respiratory surface? (b) Describe the mechanism of inspiration in human beings. Model answer: The alveolus is adapted as a respiratory surface by having a large surface area, a moist lining, a thin wall, a close association with blood capillaries, and good ventilation -- all of which maximise the rate and efficiency of gas diffusion. During inspiration, the external intercostal muscles contract (while the internal intercostals relax), lifting the ribs up and out, and the diaphragm contracts and flattens; together these increase the volume of the chest cavity, lowering the pressure inside it so that air is drawn into the lungs, where oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out into the alveoli.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 40: (a) Explain gaseous exchange in green plants during the day and at night. (b) Using named organisms, explain an industrial application of respiration. (c) Describe how gum disease develops. Model answer: During the day, a plant photosynthesises as well as respires, so it takes in more carbon dioxide than it produces and releases oxygen overall; at night, with no photosynthesis occurring, respiration alone continues, so the plant takes in oxygen and releases carbon dioxide. Industrially, yeast's anaerobic respiration (fermentation) is exploited in brewing and baking to produce alcohol and carbon dioxide; bacteria are similarly used to ferment milk into products like yoghurt and cheese, and both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria are used in sewage treatment to break down organic waste. Gum disease begins when bacteria in saliva and food residue form a sticky plaque on the teeth; if not removed, minerals deposit in the plaque to form hard tartar, and the bacteria spread beneath the gumline, causing inflammation, bleeding gums and bad breath, and, if untreated, progressing to destroy the fibres holding the tooth in place, eventually causing tooth loss.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 41: (a) Explain how HIV can be transmitted from one person to another. (b) Explain the causes of stigma faced by people living with HIV/AIDS, and ways of reducing it. Model answer: HIV can be transmitted through unprotected sexual intercourse (via semen or vaginal fluid), sharing needles during intravenous drug use, transfusion of infected blood or blood products, from mother to child during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding, organ transplantation from an infected donor, and, more rarely, sharing items like razors or toothbrushes that may carry infected blood. Stigma against people living with HIV often stems from a lack of accurate information, outdated beliefs and fear, the perception of HIV as affecting only certain groups, and limited access to support services. It can be reduced by providing antiretroviral treatment, offering counselling and genuine emotional support, actively avoiding discrimination or exclusion (in education, healthcare, housing or social settings), and correcting misinformation through public education.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 42: Explain how the internal parts of a leaf are adapted for photosynthesis. Model answer: A leaf's internal structure supports photosynthesis in several ways: a thin lamina allows light to penetrate easily to the photosynthetic cells; a broad surface area captures as much light as possible; veins (vascular bundles) supply water to the leaf via the xylem and remove the sugars produced via the phloem; stomata allow carbon dioxide in and oxygen out; and chloroplasts absorb light energy, with the highest concentration found in the palisade mesophyll (closest to the light source), somewhat fewer in the spongy mesophyll below, and fewest in the guard cells.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 43: (a) Describe the production of ATP during respiration. (b) Suggest ways respiration is important to living things. (c) Compare and contrast the respiratory surfaces of humans and fish. Model answer: ATP is produced during respiration by using the energy released from food to combine adenosine diphosphate (ADP) with an inorganic phosphate group, forming adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which the cell can then use as a ready energy source. Respiration underpins essential life processes: muscle contraction (movement, from walking to blinking), active transport of substances across cell membranes, transmission of nerve impulses (supporting senses such as hearing and sight), and cell division for growth and tissue repair. The human respiratory surface -- the alveolus -- has a large surface area, moist thin walls and a rich blood supply to maximise diffusion. The fish respiratory surface -- the gill -- is built around a gill bar carrying rows of filaments, each covered in fine lamellae rich in capillaries; gill rakers filter out solid particles before they reach the delicate filaments, and a bony flap (the operculum) protects the gills and helps regulate water flow over them.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 10 level, Question 44: (a) Define, with examples: therapeutic drugs, narcotic drugs, and recreational drugs. (b) Suggest the dangers of abusing drugs to alter body image. Model answer: Therapeutic drugs are used to treat illness by acting on cell receptors or enzymes to restore healthy function (examples include antibiotics and heart medications). Narcotic drugs act on opioid receptors in the central nervous system, commonly to relieve pain, and are typically addictive with mood- and behaviour-altering effects (examples include morphine and heroin). Recreational drugs are substances taken for pleasure or leisure rather than medical treatment (examples include alcohol, tobacco and caffeine). Drugs abused to alter body image (such as anabolic steroids or stimulants used for weight or muscle control) carry real dangers: increased risk of cardiovascular problems such as high blood pressure and heart disease, severe withdrawal effects (including nausea, tremors and, in some cases, hallucinations), impaired nervous-system function and reaction times, and associated social harms such as crime linked to sustaining the habit.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 14: (a) Explain the role of the kidney in excretion, and in homeostasis. (b) Describe the disadvantages of a kidney transplant. (c) Explain why plants have limited excretion of nitrogenous wastes and salts. Model answer: The kidney removes waste from the blood in two stages: ultrafiltration, in which small molecules (water, mineral salts, glucose and urea) are filtered from the blood in the glomerulus into the Bowman's capsule, and selective reabsorption, in which useful substances -- all the glucose, some water and some salts -- are reabsorbed back into the blood from the renal tubule, leaving urea and excess water and salts to be excreted as urine. The kidney also contributes to homeostasis through osmoregulation in the loop of Henle: when the body is short of water, the pituitary gland releases antidiuretic hormone (ADH), which increases water reabsorption from the filtrate, producing small volumes of concentrated urine; when the body has enough water, less ADH is released and more dilute urine is produced. Kidney transplants carry real disadvantages: finding a well-matched donor (living or deceased) can be difficult, the surgery is expensive, there is a risk of the recipient's immune system rejecting the new kidney (requiring lifelong immunosuppressive drugs), and some people's religious beliefs may conflict with organ transplantation. Plants excrete relatively little nitrogenous waste or salt because they use most of the nitrogen they take up to build proteins for growth, they lack a dedicated excretory system for such wastes, and their metabolism produces comparatively little nitrogenous waste in the first place.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 15: (a) What is the importance of leukaemia and sickle cell anaemia in relation to blood? (b) Explain the role of houseflies in disease transmission. (c) Discuss the cause, signs, symptoms and prevention of malaria. Model answer: Leukaemia is a cancer of the blood and bone marrow in which abnormal white blood cells are produced rapidly but fail to fight infection effectively. Sickle cell anaemia is an inherited condition in which red blood cells take on an abnormal, sickle (crescent) shape, reducing their ability to carry oxygen efficiently. Houseflies act as mechanical vectors, carrying disease-causing organisms such as those responsible for cholera, dysentery and typhoid on the outside of their bodies and depositing them on food or surfaces. ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 16: (a) Explain the importance of transpiration, and how environmental factors affect its rate. (b) Describe the role of blood in transporting materials around the body. Model answer: Transpiration helps cool leaves in hot conditions, drives a continuous supply of water to the leaves for photosynthesis and to keep cells turgid, provides the pathway by which dissolved mineral salts move through the plant, and returns water vapour to the atmosphere as part of the water cycle. Its rate is affected by humidity (higher humidity reduces the concentration gradient between leaf and air, slowing transpiration) and by wind (moving air sweeps away the layer of water vapour that builds up over a leaf's surface, speeding up transpiration). Blood serves as the body's transport system: it carries oxygen (as oxyhaemoglobin) from the lungs to the tissues, distributes digested nutrients absorbed in the gut, removes carbon dioxide and other metabolic wastes for excretion via the lungs and kidneys, and carries hormones from the glands that produce them to their target tissues.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 17: (a) Explain positive geotropism and phototropism. (b) Distinguish between a tropic response and a tactic response. (c) Outline the path taken by an impulse through a spinal reflex arc. Model answer: Positive geotropism is a plant part's growth response towards the pull of gravity (as roots do); phototropism is a plant part's growth response to light, either towards or away from it. A tropic response is a directional growth response (permanent, relatively slow, and driven by plant hormones), while a tactic response is a directional locomotory response (temporary, relatively fast, and not hormone-driven) seen in motile organisms. In a spinal reflex arc, a receptor detects a stimulus and generates an impulse, which a sensory neurone carries to the spinal cord; there a relay neurone passes the impulse to a motor neurone, which carries it on to an effector (typically a muscle), producing a rapid, automatic response.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 18: (a) Explain transpiration pull, translocation, and guttation. (b) Describe how a simple potometer can be used to demonstrate transpiration. Model answer: Transpiration pull is the continuous upward movement of water through a plant's xylem, driven by transpiration from the leaves along with capillary action and root pressure. Translocation is the movement of manufactured food (mainly sucrose and amino acids in solution) through the phloem, from where it is produced or stored to where it is needed. Guttation is the exudation of water droplets from the tips or margins of leaves through specialised pores called hydathodes, typically under conditions of high humidity and root pressure. A potometer is set up by cutting a leafy shoot underwater and fitting it tightly into water-filled tubing connected to a capillary tube dipped in a beaker of water; once assembled, an air bubble is introduced into the capillary tube, and as the plant transpires and draws up water, the bubble moves along the tube -- the time it takes to travel a measured distance gives a relative measure of the transpiration rate.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 19: (a) What is meant by the term hormone? (b) Describe the function of auxins in plants. (c) Relate the effect of auxins to geotropism. Model answer: A hormone is a chemical messenger, produced by a ductless (endocrine) gland, that travels in the blood (or, in plants, through tissues) and acts on one or more target sites before eventually being broken down. In plants, auxins promote cell elongation just behind the growing tip of shoots and roots, maintain apical dominance by suppressing the growth of lateral buds, direct the plant's growth responses to light and gravity, and delay the shedding (abscission) of leaves and fruit. In geotropism, gravity causes auxins to accumulate on the lower side of a horizontally placed shoot or root: in the shoot, the resulting extra elongation on the lower side bends it upward (away from gravity), while in the root, the same accumulation of auxin actually inhibits elongation on the lower side, causing the root to curve downward (towards gravity).

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 20: (a) Describe the structure of a synovial joint and the function of its parts. (b) Differentiate between tendon and ligament, and between a ball-and-socket joint and a hinge joint. Model answer: A synovial joint includes ligaments (joining bone to bone, stabilising the joint against dislocation), tendons (joining muscle to bone, transmitting the pull of muscle contraction into movement), a joint capsule enclosing the whole structure, a synovial membrane that secretes lubricating synovial fluid (which also nourishes the joint and reduces friction), and cartilage covering the bone ends (cushioning shocks and further reducing friction). Ligaments and tendons differ in what they connect and in composition: ligaments join bone to bone and are made of a relatively elastic protein, while tendons join muscle to bone and are made of tough, inelastic collagen. Ball-and-socket joints (such as the hip and shoulder) allow movement in multiple directions, with a rounded bone end fitting into a cup-shaped socket; hinge joints (such as the elbow and knee) allow movement in only one plane, working like a door hinge.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 21: (a) Explain the process of transpiration. (b) Describe environmental factors which affect transpiration. (c) Relate the significance of transpiration to plants and the environment. Model answer: Transpiration is the diffusion of water vapour from a leaf's internal air spaces, through the stomata (more numerous on the leaf's underside), into the atmosphere; water reaches the mesophyll cells from the xylem by osmosis before evaporating into these air spaces. Its rate rises with temperature (faster-moving water molecules diffuse out more readily), falls with humidity (a smaller concentration gradient between leaf and air slows diffusion), and rises with light intensity (which causes stomata to open wider). Transpiration matters because it cools the leaf, maintains a continuous supply of water for photosynthesis and cell turgor, carries dissolved mineral salts through the plant, and returns water vapour to the atmosphere, contributing to the wider water cycle and local humidity.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 22: (a) Using auxin theory, explain the effect of unidirectional light on shoot growth. (b) Describe taxic responses, with named examples. Model answer: When light reaches a shoot from one direction, auxin (sensitive to light) accumulates on the shaded side rather than the illuminated side; the resulting extra cell elongation on the shaded side causes the shoot to bend towards the light. A taxic response is a whole-organism movement, towards (positive) or away from (negative) a stimulus, seen in motile organisms -- for example, woodlice move towards moisture (positively hydrotactic) but away from light (negatively phototactic), earthworms move towards both moisture and soil (positively hydrotactic and geotactic), and cockroaches move away from light (negatively phototactic).

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 23: (a) Explain positive phototropism. (b) Explain the effect of gravity on the plumule and radicle of a germinating seed. (c) Compare and contrast adrenaline and glucagon. Model answer: Positive phototropism is the growth of a plant part towards a source of light. In a germinating seed lying horizontally, gravity causes auxin to accumulate on the lower sides of both the plumule (shoot) and radicle (root); in the plumule this extra auxin increases cell elongation on the lower side, curving it upward, while in the radicle the same accumulation instead inhibits elongation on the lower side, curving it downward. Glucagon and adrenaline are both hormones that raise blood glucose, but differently: glucagon, released by the pancreas when blood glucose is low, causes the liver to convert stored glycogen (and, if needed, fat and protein) into glucose; adrenaline, released by the adrenal glands in response to stress, fear or excitement, prepares the body for 'fight or flight' by also raising blood glucose alongside effects such as increased heart rate and breathing rate.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 24: (a) Distinguish between a ball-and-socket joint and a hinge joint. (b) Describe the action of antagonistic muscles in bending and straightening the arm. (c) Differentiate between thoracic and lumbar vertebrae. Model answer: A ball-and-socket joint has a rounded bone end fitting into a cup-shaped socket and allows movement in multiple directions (as at the hip and shoulder), while a hinge joint moves like a door hinge, allowing movement in only one plane (as at the elbow and knee). In the arm, bending (flexion) occurs when the biceps contracts and shortens, pulling the radius upward, while the triceps relaxes; straightening (extension) occurs when the triceps contracts and the biceps relaxes -- an antagonistic pairing in which one muscle's contraction is opposed by the other's relaxation. Thoracic and lumbar vertebrae differ in several ways: thoracic vertebrae have long, fairly thin spinous processes and facets for rib attachment, with a relatively large spinal canal and thin intervertebral discs, while lumbar vertebrae have short, blunt spinous processes and no rib facets, a comparatively smaller spinal canal, and thick, massive intervertebral discs suited to bearing the greater load of the upper body.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 25: (a) Explain the functions of the Eustachian tube and cochlea in the human ear. (b) Distinguish between sensory and motor neurones. (c) Describe the pupil reflex. Model answer: The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the back of the throat and opens during swallowing or yawning to equalise air pressure on either side of the eardrum, protecting it from damage during rapid pressure changes (such as in an aircraft). The cochlea, a coiled, fluid-filled structure in the inner ear, detects the pitch and loudness of sound and converts these vibrations into nerve impulses, carried to the brain via the auditory nerve. Sensory neurones carry impulses from receptors to the central nervous system and typically have long dendrites and a short axon, with the cell body positioned off the main nerve fibre; motor neurones carry impulses from the central nervous system to effectors and have a long axon and short dendrites, with the cell body at one end. ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 26: (a) What is meant by excretion? (b) Explain how carbon dioxide and urea are excreted from the body. Model answer: Excretion is the removal from the body of toxic waste products of metabolism. Carbon dioxide, a waste product of respiration, is excreted by the lungs: it diffuses from the blood into the alveoli across the thin alveolar walls and is breathed out. Urea, produced from the breakdown of excess amino acids, is excreted by the kidneys in a two-stage process: ultrafiltration, in which blood pressure forces small molecules (water, salts, glucose and urea) out of the glomerulus into the Bowman's capsule as glomerular filtrate; and selective reabsorption, in which useful substances -- all the glucose, most of the water, and needed salts -- are reabsorbed back into the blood as the filtrate passes along the renal tubule (glucose by diffusion, water by osmosis, salts by active transport), leaving urea, excess water and excess salts to be excreted as urine via the collecting duct, ureter and bladder.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 27: (a) Explain the term homeostasis. (b) Discuss the role of the liver in homeostasis. Model answer: Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment, encompassing processes such as thermoregulation (body temperature), osmoregulation (water balance), blood sugar regulation, and excretion of metabolic wastes. The liver plays a central role in blood sugar regulation: when blood glucose rises, insulin (from the pancreas) stimulates liver cells to convert excess glucose into glycogen for storage and to oxidise glucose for energy, lowering blood glucose; when blood glucose falls, glucagon stimulates the liver to convert stored glycogen back into glucose, and to convert fats and amino acids into glucose if needed. The liver's many metabolic reactions, together with its dense network of blood vessels, also make it an important source of body heat.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 28: (a) Describe the process of seed germination. (b) Explain how auxins affect the growth of shoots. Model answer: Germination begins when a seed absorbs water, activating enzymes that mobilise stored food reserves to nourish the growing embryo; the radicle (embryonic root) emerges first and grows downward, anchoring the seedling and beginning to absorb water and nutrients from the soil, while the plumule (embryonic shoot) grows upward towards the light, eventually breaking through the seed coat and soil surface to begin photosynthesising on its own. Auxins affect shoot growth in response to light by accumulating on the shaded side of the shoot (moving away from the illuminated side); the resulting greater cell elongation on that shaded side causes the shoot to bend towards the light source.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 29: (a) Explain what is meant by double circulation in humans. (b) Distinguish between its two parts. (c) Discuss the transport functions of blood. Model answer: Double circulation means blood passes through the heart twice for each complete circuit of the body, via two connected circuits: pulmonary circulation and systemic circulation. In pulmonary circulation, deoxygenated blood is pumped from the heart to the lungs at relatively low pressure to be oxygenated; in systemic circulation, oxygenated blood is pumped from the heart to the rest of the body at much higher pressure. Blood performs several transport functions: carrying oxygen (as oxyhaemoglobin) from the lungs to body tissues, distributing digested nutrients from the gut, removing carbon dioxide and other wastes for excretion, carrying hormones from glands to target tissues, helping regulate body temperature by varying blood flow to the skin, defending against infection via white blood cells, and limiting blood loss through clotting.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 30: (a) Explain the causes of coronary heart disease, and how it can be prevented. (b) Describe the function of lymph nodes in disease prevention. Model answer: Coronary heart disease is linked to a diet high in fatty foods (which raises blood cholesterol and can block the coronary arteries), smoking (nicotine promotes cholesterol build-up), emotional stress (raised adrenaline similarly promotes cholesterol accumulation), a sedentary lifestyle, and exposure to pollutants such as carbon monoxide. It can be reduced by cutting down on fatty foods, exercising regularly (which improves circulation and reduces cholesterol build-up), and avoiding smoking and chronic stress. Lymph nodes help prevent disease by producing and storing lymphocytes (added to the lymph as it drains towards the bloodstream) and by filtering out bacteria, foreign material and dead tissue from the lymph before it rejoins the blood -- becoming swollen and tender when they are actively fighting an infection.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 31: Describe the characteristics and functions of the vertebral column, skull, and skeletal muscle. Model answer: The vertebral column is made up of individual vertebrae, each with a neural arch enclosing a canal (the neural canal, protecting the spinal cord) and projections for muscle and rib attachment; it supports the trunk, protects the spinal cord, and, through its cartilaginous joints, allows flexibility. The skull consists of fused bony plates forming the cranium, plus facial bones (including the nasal bones, cheekbones and eye sockets); its main functions are protecting the brain from injury and housing and protecting the sense organs of the head. Skeletal muscle is made of long, striated muscle fibres bundled together, richly supplied with blood vessels and nerves, and typically arranged in antagonistic pairs; it produces voluntary movement (from walking to fine motor tasks) by contracting and pulling on the bones it is attached to via tendons.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 32: (a) What is meant by tropic response? (b) Discuss the role of auxins in a shoot receiving light from one side, and in a radicle growing horizontally. Model answer: A tropic response is the directional growth of a plant part towards (positive) or away from (negative) a stimulus such as light or gravity. In a shoot lit from one side, auxin accumulates on the shaded side, causing greater elongation there and bending the shoot towards the light. In a radicle growing horizontally, gravity causes auxin to accumulate on the lower side; but unlike in shoots, this auxin accumulation inhibits cell elongation in the root, so the upper side elongates more and the radicle curves downward, towards gravity.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 33: (a) Explain how flowering plants get rid of excess and unwanted substances. (b) Discuss the importance of homeostasis in mammals. Model answer: Flowering plants remove excess water through transpiration via the stomata, exchange carbon dioxide and oxygen by diffusion through the same stomata, dispose of other wastes by storing them in leaves that are later shed, and release substances such as resins and gums as exudates. In mammals, homeostasis is vital for survival: it regulates body temperature (avoiding damage from overheating or excessive cooling), maintains stable blood glucose levels (via insulin and glucagon), helps the body resist infection, keeps blood pressure within a safe range, regulates the water and ion balance of body fluids, supports steady, adequate breathing to meet oxygen needs, and ensures the continuous removal of metabolic wastes and toxins.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 34: (a) Describe the structure and function of endocrine glands, and the difference between the adrenal and salivary glands. (b) Explain the function of insulin and antidiuretic hormone (ADH). Model answer: Endocrine glands are ductless glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream; examples include the pancreas (insulin and glucagon), the adrenal glands (adrenaline) and the thyroid gland (thyroxine). The adrenal gland is a true endocrine gland, releasing adrenaline into the blood in response to fear or stress to prepare the body for 'fight or flight'; the salivary gland, by contrast, is an exocrine gland, releasing saliva through a duct into the mouth in response to the smell or presence of food, to aid digestion. Insulin, released when blood glucose is high, causes liver and muscle cells to convert excess glucose into glycogen for storage (and excess glucose into fat), lowering blood glucose. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH), released by the pituitary gland when the body is short of water, increases water reabsorption in the kidney's collecting ducts, producing a smaller volume of more concentrated urine.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 35: (a) State the parts of a synovial joint and their functions. (b) Explain why bone is a living tissue. (c) Explain the antagonistic action of the eye's iris muscles moving from dark to bright light. Model answer: A synovial joint's parts include ligaments (bone to bone, preventing dislocation), tendons (muscle to bone, transmitting movement), a joint capsule enclosing the joint, a synovial membrane secreting lubricating synovial fluid, and cartilage cushioning the bone ends. Bone is a living tissue because it contains living cells within a network of blood vessels that keep those cells alive and support growth and repair; its spongy interior contains red bone marrow (which produces red blood cells), and its central cavity contains yellow marrow (associated with fat storage and some white blood cell production). Moving from a dark room into bright light, the circular muscles of the iris contract while the radial muscles relax, constricting the pupil to limit the amount of light reaching the retina and prevent it from being overwhelmed.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 36: (a) Explain why blood group AB is the universal recipient, and why a person with blood group A cannot donate to a person with blood group B. (b) Explain the importance of precautions taken before a blood transfusion. Model answer: Blood group AB is called the universal recipient because its plasma contains no anti-A or anti-B antibodies, so it will not attack the antigens present in blood from any other group, avoiding the clumping (agglutination) that would otherwise occur. A person with blood group A cannot safely donate to someone with blood group B because the recipient's plasma contains anti-A antibodies, which would attack the A antigens on the donor's red blood cells and cause agglutination. Before a transfusion, blood must be carefully screened and matched: the donor's ABO and rhesus blood group must match (or be compatible with) the recipient's, and the blood must be tested for infections such as hepatitis and HIV -- precautions essential to prevent a fatal transfusion reaction or transmitting disease.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 37: (a) Define homeostasis. (b) Briefly explain the roles of the kidney and liver in homeostasis. (c) Describe when and how the skin reduces heat loss from the body. Model answer: Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment. The kidney contributes through osmoregulation, adjusting how much water and salt are reabsorbed from the filtrate (partly under the control of antidiuretic hormone) to keep body fluid concentration constant. The liver contributes mainly through blood sugar regulation, converting excess glucose to glycogen when levels are high and converting glycogen back to glucose when levels are low, while its metabolic activity also generates body heat. In cold conditions, the skin reduces heat loss by contracting tiny erector muscles to raise body hairs (trapping an insulating layer of air, seen as goose bumps), by vasoconstriction (narrowing blood vessels near the skin surface to reduce blood flow and heat loss there), by diverting blood through deeper shunt vessels away from the skin surface, and by reducing sweat gland activity to minimise evaporative heat loss.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 38: (a) Distinguish between open and closed blood circulatory systems. (b) Explain single and double circulation, with examples. (c) Compare and contrast arteries and veins. Model answer: In an open circulatory system, blood (haemolymph) flows freely through body cavities rather than being confined to vessels, moving at low pressure from a simple, sac-like heart; in a closed circulatory system, blood remains within vessels throughout and is pumped at higher pressure. Single circulation, seen in fish, involves blood passing through the heart only once per full circuit (heart to gills to body and back to heart); double circulation, seen in mammals, involves blood passing through the heart twice per circuit -- once through the pulmonary circuit to the lungs and back, and once through the systemic circuit to the rest of the body and back. Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure, with thick muscular walls and narrow lumens (and, except for the pulmonary artery, carry oxygenated blood); veins carry blood back to the heart at low pressure, with thinner walls, wider lumens, and valves to prevent backflow (and, except for the pulmonary vein, carry deoxygenated blood).

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 39: (a) Explain the process of transpiration. (b) Describe environmental factors which affect transpiration. (c) Relate the significance of transpiration to plants and the environment. Model answer: Transpiration is the diffusion of water vapour from a leaf's internal air spaces, through its stomata, into the atmosphere, with water reaching those air spaces from the xylem via the mesophyll cells by osmosis and evaporation. Higher temperature speeds up transpiration (faster-moving water molecules diffuse out more readily); higher humidity slows it (a smaller concentration gradient between leaf and air); and higher light intensity speeds it up (by causing stomata to open wider). Transpiration is significant because it cools the leaf, drives the continuous uptake of water needed for photosynthesis and turgor, carries dissolved mineral salts through the plant, and returns water vapour to the atmosphere as part of the broader water cycle.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 11 level, Question 40: (a) Describe the functions of the main components of blood. (b) Explain sickle cell anaemia and haemophilia as blood disorders. Model answer: Blood's components each serve a distinct role: red blood cells carry oxygen (as oxyhaemoglobin); white blood cells defend against infection; platelets trigger clotting to limit blood loss; and plasma carries dissolved nutrients, hormones, and waste products (including carbon dioxide) for distribution or removal. Sickle cell anaemia is an inherited condition in which abnormal haemoglobin causes red blood cells to become sickle-shaped, especially when oxygen levels are low, reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. Haemophilia is an inherited condition, caused by the absence of specific blood-clotting factors, in which affected individuals bleed for abnormally long periods after injury because their blood cannot clot properly.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 22: (a) What are the advantages of sexual reproduction over asexual reproduction in flowering plants? (b) Explain how asexual reproduction occurs in a fungus such as Rhizopus. (c) Describe the sequence of events from pollination to fertilisation in a flower. Model answer: Sexual reproduction in flowering plants produces genetically varied offspring (since it combines genetic material from two parents), which increases a population's ability to adapt to changing conditions and disease, and it also enables the production of seeds that can survive dormant periods and disperse to colonise new areas -- advantages asexual reproduction, which produces genetically identical clones, does not share. Rhizopus (a bread mould) reproduces asexually by producing sporangia -- rounded structures on upright hyphae -- inside which large numbers of spores develop; when mature, the sporangia release these spores, which are dispersed (often by air) and germinate into new fungal colonies wherever conditions are favourable. In a flower, pollination -- the transfer of pollen from anther to stigma -- is followed by the germination of the pollen grain, which grows a pollen tube down through the style to the ovary; a male gamete travels down this tube and fuses with the egg cell in the ovule (fertilisation), after which the ovule develops into a seed and the ovary into a fruit.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 23: (a) Explain the effects of fishing and charcoal burning on other organisms. (b) Describe the undesirable effects of raw sewage in water and sulphur dioxide in air. Model answer: Overfishing can deplete fish stocks faster than they can reproduce, disrupting food chains that depend on those fish and threatening the livelihoods of communities that rely on fishing. Charcoal burning requires cutting down trees, which destroys habitat for the organisms that depend on that woodland, contributes to soil erosion once the protective tree cover is removed, and reduces the vegetation available to absorb carbon dioxide. Raw sewage discharged into water encourages the rapid growth of bacteria and algae that consume the water's dissolved oxygen as they decompose the waste, suffocating fish and other aquatic organisms, and can also spread disease-causing organisms to people who use the water. Sulphur dioxide in the air combines with atmospheric moisture to form acid rain, which damages plant tissue, acidifies soils and water bodies (harming aquatic life), and corrodes buildings; when inhaled, it also irritates the human respiratory system.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 24: (a) Describe how nitrogen is cycled within an ecosystem. (b) Describe the importance of the physical components of soil. Model answer: In the nitrogen cycle, nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living in the soil or living symbiotically in the root nodules of legumes) convert atmospheric nitrogen gas into ammonium compounds that plants can use; nitrifying bacteria further convert ammonium into nitrites and then nitrates, the form in which plants mainly absorb nitrogen to build proteins. Animals obtain nitrogen by eating plants (or other animals), and when organisms die or excrete waste, decomposers break down the nitrogen-containing compounds, releasing ammonia back into the soil to be nitrified again; denitrifying bacteria complete the cycle by converting some soil nitrates back into atmospheric nitrogen gas. Soil's physical components each matter: mineral particles (sand, silt, clay) determine drainage and water-holding capacity and anchor plant roots; organic matter (humus) improves fertility and structure; air spaces between particles allow root and soil-organism respiration; and water held in the soil is essential for plant uptake and for dissolving and transporting nutrients.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 25: (a) Describe the special dietary needs of a pregnant woman. (b) Discuss artificial methods of birth control. Model answer: A pregnant woman needs extra protein to support the growth of the foetus and her own tissues, additional iron and folic acid to support increased blood volume and prevent neural tube defects in the developing baby, extra calcium for the foetus's developing bones and teeth, and generally more energy (calories) to meet the raised metabolic demands of pregnancy, alongside a good general balance of vitamins and minerals. Artificial birth control methods include barrier methods (such as condoms, which also protect against sexually transmitted infections), hormonal methods (such as the contraceptive pill or injectable contraceptives, which prevent ovulation), intrauterine devices (which prevent implantation), and permanent surgical methods (such as tubal ligation or vasectomy) -- each varying in effectiveness, reversibility and side effects.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 26: (a) With the aid of a labelled diagram, describe the carbon cycle. (b) Discuss the effects of deforestation on the carbon cycle. Model answer: In the carbon cycle, plants remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere through photosynthesis, using it to build carbohydrates and other organic molecules; this carbon passes to animals when they eat plants (or other animals), and is returned to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide when organisms respire, or when decomposers break down dead organic matter and waste. Additional carbon dioxide is released by the combustion of fossil fuels and by volcanic activity, while some carbon is locked away for long periods in fossil fuels, limestone and ocean sediments. Deforestation disrupts this cycle by removing trees that would otherwise absorb carbon dioxide, releasing the carbon stored in cut or burned trees back into the atmosphere, and reducing the land's overall capacity to act as a carbon sink -- all of which contribute to rising atmospheric carbon dioxide levels and, in turn, to climate change.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 27: (a) Using named examples, explain how fruits and seeds are adapted for dispersal. (b) Explain the advantages and disadvantages of vegetative propagation. Model answer: Fruits and seeds show adaptations matched to their dispersal method: wind-dispersed seeds, such as those of grasses or the winged fruits of species like the sycamore-type tree, are light with structures that increase drag or lift; animal-dispersed fruits are often fleshy and attractively coloured to encourage animals to eat them and pass the seeds out elsewhere in their droppings, or are covered in hooks (like burrs) that catch on fur or clothing; water-dispersed seeds, such as the coconut, are buoyant and waterproofed to survive floating over long distances; and some fruits, like those of certain legumes, dry out and split explosively to fling their seeds away from the parent plant. Vegetative propagation (producing new plants from a parent's roots, stems or leaves rather than seeds) has the advantage of producing genetically identical offspring quickly, without needing pollination, and preserving desirable characteristics exactly -- but its disadvantage is that it produces no genetic variation, leaving the resulting population equally vulnerable to the same diseases or environmental stresses.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 28: (a) Describe grafting and budding as methods of artificial vegetative propagation. (b) Describe artificial methods of birth control in humans. Model answer: Grafting joins a cutting (the scion) from one plant onto the rooted stem (the rootstock) of another, so that their tissues fuse and grow as a single plant combining the rootstock's hardy root system with the scion's desired fruit or flower characteristics. Budding is a similar technique in which a single bud, rather than a full cutting, is inserted under the bark of the rootstock and allowed to fuse and grow into a new shoot. Artificial birth control methods in humans include barrier methods (condoms), hormonal methods (the contraceptive pill or injections, which suppress ovulation), intrauterine devices (which prevent implantation of a fertilised egg), and permanent surgical methods (vasectomy in men, tubal ligation in women).

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 29: (a) Explain the terms pollination and fertilisation. (b) Discuss fruit and seed dispersal. Model answer: Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther of a flower to the stigma (of the same or another flower of the same species); fertilisation is the subsequent fusion of a male gamete (carried down the pollen tube) with the egg cell inside the ovule, producing a zygote that develops into an embryo. Fruits and seeds are dispersed away from the parent plant to reduce competition for light, water and nutrients between parent and offspring, and to colonise new areas; dispersal mechanisms include wind (light, often winged or feathery seeds), animals (fleshy fruits eaten and excreted elsewhere, or hooked fruits carried on fur), water (buoyant, waterproof seeds), and self-dispersal (explosive seed pods that fling seeds away mechanically).

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 30: (a) Explain the terms community, habitat and niche. (b) Describe the role of producers, consumers and decomposers within an ecosystem. Model answer: A community is all the different populations of organisms living and interacting together in a particular area; a habitat is the specific place where an organism normally lives; and a niche is the particular role an organism plays within its habitat and community, including what it feeds on, how it interacts with other species, and the conditions it requires. Producers (green plants and algae) manufacture their own food through photosynthesis, forming the base of virtually all food chains; consumers (animals) feed on producers or on other consumers, transferring energy and matter through the ecosystem; and decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down dead organisms and waste, returning nutrients such as carbon and nitrogen to the soil and atmosphere for producers to use again.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 31: (a) Explain how flowering plants get rid of excess and unwanted substances from their bodies. (b) Discuss the importance of homeostasis in mammals. Model answer: Flowering plants remove excess water by transpiration through the stomata, exchange carbon dioxide and oxygen by diffusion (also mainly through the stomata), and dispose of some other waste products by storing them in leaves that are later shed, or by releasing them as exudates such as resins and gums. In mammals, homeostasis -- the maintenance of a stable internal environment -- is essential to survival: it keeps body temperature, blood glucose, blood pressure, and the water and ion balance of body fluids within the narrow ranges cells need to function, supports steady breathing to meet oxygen demand, helps the body resist infection, and ensures continuous removal of metabolic wastes -- all of which allow cells and organs to keep working normally regardless of changes in the external environment.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 32: (a) Describe factors that make soil fertile. (b) Explain the causes of loss of soil fertility. (c) Explain methods of improving and retaining soil fertility. Model answer: Fertile soil typically has a good supply of organic matter (humus), a balanced mineral nutrient content (especially nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium), good structure allowing air and water to move freely, a near-neutral pH suited to most crops, and a healthy population of soil organisms that help recycle nutrients. Soil fertility is lost through continuous cropping without replenishing nutrients, erosion by wind or water (which strips away the fertile topsoil), leaching of nutrients by heavy rainfall, overgrazing (which removes protective vegetation cover), and burning of vegetation, which destroys organic matter. Fertility can be improved and maintained through crop rotation (including nitrogen-fixing legumes), applying organic manure or chemical fertiliser, practices that reduce erosion (contour ploughing, terracing, cover cropping), controlled grazing, and fallowing land periodically to let it recover naturally.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 33: (a) Explain the advantages and disadvantages of vegetative propagation. (b) Describe the process of fertilisation in flowering plants. Model answer: Vegetative propagation lets growers produce new plants quickly, without needing pollination, while exactly preserving a parent plant's desirable characteristics -- but because it produces genetically identical offspring, the resulting population lacks genetic variation and is equally vulnerable to the same pests, diseases or environmental stresses. In flowering plants, fertilisation follows pollination: a pollen grain that has landed on a compatible stigma germinates, growing a pollen tube down through the style towards the ovary; the tube delivers a male gamete to an ovule, where it fuses with the egg cell to form a zygote, while a second fusion event (in flowering plants) forms the nutritive endosperm -- after fertilisation, the ovule develops into a seed and the surrounding ovary into a fruit.

Model answer: A pond ecosystem includes abiotic (non-living) features such as water, dissolved oxygen, light, temperature and the mineral nutrients dissolved in the water, alongside biotic (living) components: producers such as algae and pondweed, primary consumers such as tadpoles and small invertebrates that feed on them, secondary consumers such as fish and frogs, and decomposers that break down dead organic matter on the pond floor, recycling nutrients back into the water. Agriculture affects natural ecosystems by clearing existing vegetation to create farmland (reducing habitat and biodiversity), altering soil structure and nutrient content through ploughing and fertiliser use, potentially polluting nearby water bodies with fertiliser or pesticide runoff, and, where irrigation or livestock grazing is involved, changing local water availability and vegetation cover.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 36: (a) Explain the functions of sensory, motor and relay neurones. (b) Describe spinal reflex action and conditioned reflex action, with examples. Model answer: Sensory neurones carry nerve impulses from receptors (sense organs) to the central nervous system; relay neurones, found within the spinal cord and brain, connect sensory and motor neurones (and other relay neurones), coordinating the response; and motor neurones carry impulses from the central nervous system out to effectors such as muscles or glands. A spinal reflex action is a rapid, automatic, unlearned response that does not require conscious brain involvement -- for example, quickly withdrawing a hand from a hot object. ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 37: (a) Describe the antagonistic action of muscles in the iris. (b) Compare and contrast exoskeleton and endoskeleton. (c) Describe one joint disorder. Model answer: The iris contains two sets of antagonistic muscles: circular muscles, which contract to constrict the pupil in bright light, and radial muscles, which contract (while the circular muscles relax) to dilate the pupil in dim light -- together regulating how much light reaches the retina. An exoskeleton (as in insects) is a hard external covering that protects internal organs and provides attachment points for muscles from the outside, but must be periodically shed (moulted) to allow growth; an endoskeleton (as in vertebrates) is an internal, living, growing framework of bone or cartilage that supports the body from within and, unlike an exoskeleton, grows continuously with the animal. Arthritis is a common joint disorder in which inflammation of the joint (often due to wear of the cartilage, as in osteoarthritis) causes pain, stiffness and swelling, and can progressively reduce joint mobility.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 38: (a) Define the term biodiversity. (b) State the importance of biodiversity in an area. (c) Describe the economic reasons for maintaining biodiversity. Model answer: Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms -- species, genetic variation within species, and the range of ecosystems -- found in a given area. It matters because it supports ecosystem stability and resilience, maintains the complex food webs and ecological interactions (such as pollination and nutrient cycling) that ecosystems depend on, and preserves genetic resources that may prove valuable in future (for breeding hardier crops, for example). Economically, biodiversity underpins tourism (wildlife and nature-based tourism, important to countries like Zambia), provides a source of new medicines derived from plant and animal compounds, sustains fisheries and other harvested wild resources, and supports the pollination and natural pest control that agriculture depends on.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (Section B Essay), Grade 12 level, Question 39: (a) Explain the term population. (b) Discuss factors that cause changes in population size. Model answer: In biology, a population is all the individuals of one species living in a particular area at a particular time, capable of interbreeding with one another. Population size changes in response to birth rate and death rate (a population grows when births exceed deaths, and shrinks when the reverse is true), immigration and emigration (movement of individuals into or out of the area), availability of resources such as food, water and space (which can limit growth once they become scarce), predation and disease (which can suppress population growth), and, for human populations, factors such as access to healthcare, family planning and economic conditions.

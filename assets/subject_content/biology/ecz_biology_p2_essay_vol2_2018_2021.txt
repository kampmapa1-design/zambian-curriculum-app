ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2018 (July, G.C.E.), Question 6: (a) Distinguish between a monocot seed and a dicot seed. (b) Describe ways respiration is important to living things and the biosphere, and its industrial application in brewing and sewage treatment. Model answer: A monocot seed (such as maize) has a single seed leaf (cotyledon), typically stores its food reserve separately in an endosperm, and shows parallel-veined leaves on germination; a dicot seed (such as bean) has two cotyledons, which themselves usually store the food reserve, and produces net-veined leaves on germination. Respiration matters to living things because it releases the energy needed for growth, movement, active transport and all other life processes, and, at the level of the biosphere, respiring organisms are part of the carbon cycle, releasing carbon dioxide that plants take up again in photosynthesis. Industrially, the anaerobic respiration (fermentation) of yeast is exploited in brewing to produce alcohol from sugars, and both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria are used in sewage treatment to break down organic waste, removing pollutants before water is released back into the environment.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2018 (July, G.C.E.), Question 7: (a) Explain the meaning of the term 'hormone'. (b) Compare and contrast the nervous and endocrine systems. (c) Describe the role of the skin as a sense organ. Model answer: A hormone is a chemical messenger produced by an endocrine (ductless) gland, carried in the blood, and acting on one or more target organs before eventually being broken down. The nervous system transmits information as fast electrical impulses along nerves, producing responses that are rapid but typically short-lived and localised; the endocrine system instead sends chemical messages through the bloodstream, which travel more slowly but can produce widespread, longer-lasting effects. The skin acts as a sense organ through receptors embedded in it that detect touch, pressure, pain and temperature, sending this information to the brain via sensory neurones -- allowing the body to respond appropriately to its physical environment.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2018 (July, G.C.E.), Question 8: (a) Explain the term 'growth'. (b) Describe the conditions necessary for germination. (c) Distinguish between epigeal and hypogeal germination. Model answer: Growth is a permanent increase in the size, mass or number of cells of an organism. Seed germination requires adequate water (to activate enzymes and mobilise stored food reserves), a suitable temperature (for enzyme activity), and oxygen (for aerobic respiration to release the energy needed for growth); some seeds also require light or darkness, or a period of dormancy, to germinate. In epigeal germination, the cotyledons are pushed up above the soil surface as the seedling grows (as in beans); in hypogeal germination, the cotyledons remain below the soil surface while only the shoot emerges above ground (as in maize and peas).

(c) Discuss the causes of stigma towards people living with HIV/AIDS, and ways of reducing discrimination against them. Model answer: Having multiple sexual partners significantly raises the risk of contracting and spreading sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, and increases the chance of unintended pregnancy. Safer sexual practices include consistent and correct condom use, limiting the number of sexual partners, regular HIV/STI testing, mutual faithfulness between partners, and abstinence where risk is uncertain. Stigma towards people living with HIV/AIDS often stems from a lack of accurate information, fear, and outdated or moralistic beliefs about how the disease is contracted; it can be reduced through public education correcting misinformation, providing accessible antiretroviral treatment and counselling, and actively avoiding discriminatory behaviour such as exclusion from school, work or social life.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2018 (July, G.C.E.), Question 10: (a) Describe how carbon is recycled within an ecosystem. (b) Explain how the accumulation of non-biodegradable materials like plastics can be prevented. Model answer: Carbon is recycled through the carbon cycle: plants remove atmospheric carbon dioxide via photosynthesis and use it to build organic molecules; this carbon is passed along food chains as organisms feed on one another, and returned to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide through respiration and through the decomposition of dead organisms and waste by decomposers, with additional carbon dioxide released by the combustion of fossil fuels. The build-up of non-biodegradable plastics can be reduced by recycling and reusing plastic materials, reducing single-use plastic consumption, favouring biodegradable alternatives where available, and improving waste collection and disposal systems to prevent plastics accumulating in the environment.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2020 (G.C.E.), Question 7: (a) Explain the components of blood plasma. (b) Investigate and describe common blood disorders. Model answer: Blood plasma is mostly water, in which are dissolved plasma proteins (such as fibrinogen, involved in clotting, and albumin, which helps maintain blood's osmotic pressure), digested nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fats), hormones, antibodies, and dissolved waste products such as urea and carbon dioxide, all being transported around the body. Common blood disorders include anaemia (a shortage of red blood cells or haemoglobin, reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity), sickle cell anaemia (an inherited disorder producing abnormally shaped red blood cells), haemophilia (an inherited failure of normal blood clotting), and leukaemia (a cancer of the blood-forming tissues causing overproduction of abnormal white blood cells).

Model answer: Malaria is caused by the protozoan parasite Plasmodium. Its signs and symptoms include recurring fever, chills and sweating, headache, muscle and joint pain, fatigue, and, in severe cases, anaemia. ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2020 (G.C.E.), Question 9: (a) With named examples, describe different types of skeletons. (b) Explain the functions of the vertebral column. Model answer: Animals show three main skeleton types: a hydrostatic skeleton, found in soft-bodied invertebrates like earthworms, in which fluid-filled body cavities are squeezed by surrounding muscles to produce movement; an exoskeleton, found in insects and other arthropods, a rigid external covering that supports and protects the body but must be periodically shed to allow growth; and an endoskeleton, found in vertebrates such as humans, an internal, living framework of bone and cartilage that grows with the animal. ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2020, Question 6: (a) Explain the stages involved in testing a piece of onion for reducing sugars. (b) Explain the nutritional disorders goitre, kwashiorkor, and leaf chlorosis. Model answer: To test onion tissue for reducing sugars, a small piece is crushed or ground to release its cell contents, mixed with water, and heated with Benedict's solution in a water bath; a colour change from blue through green, yellow and orange to a brick-red precipitate indicates the presence of reducing sugar, with the intensity of colour change roughly indicating how much sugar is present. Goitre is a swelling of the thyroid gland caused by iodine deficiency, which impairs the production of thyroxine. Kwashiorkor is a severe protein-deficiency disease in children, causing swelling (oedema), a distended abdomen, and stunted growth. Leaf chlorosis is the yellowing of plant leaves, most often caused by a deficiency of magnesium or nitrogen, both needed for chlorophyll production.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2020, Question 7: (a) Describe the process of reproduction in a frog. (b) Explain how reproduction in frogs differs from that in humans. Model answer: Frogs reproduce through external fertilisation: during amplexus, the male clasps the female and releases sperm over the eggs as she lays them in water, where fertilisation occurs outside the body; the resulting eggs develop into free-swimming, gill-breathing tadpoles, which undergo metamorphosis (developing legs, lungs and losing their tail) into adult frogs. This differs from human reproduction, which involves internal fertilisation within the female's body, a much smaller number of offspring produced per reproductive cycle, and direct development of a foetus inside the mother (rather than a larval stage and external metamorphosis) before live birth.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2020, Question 9: (a) Explain primary and secondary growth in plants. (b) Explain the differences between tropisms and taxic responses. Model answer: Primary growth in plants is the increase in length of roots and shoots, driven by cell division at the apical meristems (growing tips); secondary growth is the increase in girth (thickness) of stems and roots, driven by cell division in the lateral meristems (such as the vascular cambium), producing woody tissue in many plants. A tropism is a directional growth response of a fixed organism (such as a plant) towards or away from a stimulus like light or gravity, and is relatively slow and permanent; a taxic response is a whole-body movement of a motile organism towards or away from a stimulus, and is typically much faster and does not involve growth.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2020, Question 10: (a) Explain the ecological terms abiotic factors and niche. (b) Describe the key features of a water-body ecosystem such as a lake, river or pond. Model answer: Abiotic factors are the non-living physical and chemical components of an environment -- such as temperature, light, water and dissolved oxygen -- that influence which organisms can live there; a niche is the specific role an organism occupies within its ecosystem, including its feeding relationships and habitat requirements. A water-body ecosystem such as a pond includes abiotic features (light penetration, temperature, dissolved oxygen and nutrients) alongside producers such as algae and aquatic plants, primary consumers such as small invertebrates and tadpoles, secondary consumers such as fish, and decomposers that break down dead organic matter on the bottom, recycling nutrients back into the water.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2021 (G.C.E.), Question 6: (a) Define aerobic respiration. (b) Describe the production of ATP in living cells. (c) Compare and contrast respiration in plants and humans. Model answer: Aerobic respiration is the release of a relatively large amount of energy from food substances in living cells, using oxygen and producing carbon dioxide and water as by-products. ATP is produced by using the energy released during respiration to combine adenosine diphosphate (ADP) with an inorganic phosphate group, forming adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the cell's immediate energy currency. Respiration in plants and humans shares the same basic biochemistry (breaking down glucose using oxygen to release energy, carbon dioxide and water), but differs in gas exchange: plants exchange gases passively through stomata (and, at night, respiration dominates over photosynthesis, so plants are net carbon dioxide producers), while humans actively ventilate their lungs to bring in oxygen and expel carbon dioxide continuously.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2021 (G.C.E.), Question 7: (a) Describe the internal structure of the heart. (b) Explain the causes of coronary heart disease, and how it can be prevented. Model answer: The heart has four chambers: two upper atria, which receive blood returning to the heart, and two lower, thicker-walled ventricles, which pump blood out; the right side handles deoxygenated blood (received from the vena cava, pumped to the lungs via the pulmonary artery) while the left side handles oxygenated blood (received from the pulmonary vein, pumped to the body via the aorta); valves between the chambers, and at the exits of the ventricles, prevent backflow of blood. Coronary heart disease develops mainly from cholesterol build-up in the coronary arteries (worsened by a diet high in fatty foods), smoking (nicotine promotes this build-up), chronic stress, and lack of exercise; it can be prevented by eating a lower-fat diet, exercising regularly, and avoiding smoking and excessive stress.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2021 (G.C.E.), Question 8: (a) Explain cranial, conditioned and spinal reflexes, with named examples. (b) Explain why cranial and conditioned reflexes are important. (c) Describe how an impulse is transmitted from one neurone to another. Model answer: A spinal reflex is a rapid, automatic, unlearned response coordinated by the spinal cord, such as withdrawing a hand from a hot object. A cranial reflex is similarly automatic and unlearned but coordinated by the brain rather than the spinal cord, such as the pupil reflex to changing light. A conditioned reflex is a learned response, built up through repeated association between a stimulus and an outcome, such as a person's mouth watering at the sight (rather than the taste) of food. Cranial reflexes are important because they allow rapid, protective responses (like blinking or adjusting pupil size) without needing conscious thought; conditioned reflexes are important because they let an organism anticipate and prepare for events based on past experience. Between neurones, an impulse crosses the gap (synapse) chemically: the arriving impulse triggers the release of a neurotransmitter from the first neurone, which diffuses across the synaptic gap and binds to receptors on the next neurone, triggering a new electrical impulse there.

Model answer: Conservation is the careful management and protection of natural resources -- including plant and animal species and their habitats -- to prevent their depletion or extinction. ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2021, Question 6: (a) Describe the movement of organic solutes in phloem tissue. (b) Outline how a potometer is used to measure transpiration rate. (c) Explain the factors that affect transpiration rate. Model answer: In phloem tissue, dissolved organic solutes -- mainly sucrose and amino acids -- move by translocation from a 'source' (where they are made or stored, such as a photosynthesising leaf) to a 'sink' (where they are used or stored, such as a growing root or fruit), a process that can move materials both up and down the plant, unlike the one-way upward flow in the xylem. A potometer measures transpiration rate indirectly: a leafy shoot is fixed into water-filled tubing connected to a capillary tube in a beaker of water; an air bubble introduced into the capillary tube moves along it as the plant draws up water to replace what it loses by transpiration, and the time taken for the bubble to travel a set distance gives a measure of the transpiration rate. Transpiration rate rises with higher temperature and light intensity (both of which speed water loss and, for light, open the stomata wider) and with greater wind speed (which removes the humid air layer next to the leaf), but falls as humidity rises (reducing the concentration gradient driving diffusion).

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2021, Question 9: (a) Describe the functions of thyroxine and glucagon. (b) Explain the causes of short sight and how it can be corrected. (c) Suggest ways of preventing deafness. Model answer: Thyroxine, produced by the thyroid gland, regulates the body's metabolic rate, influencing growth and energy use; glucagon, produced by the pancreas, raises blood glucose levels by stimulating the liver to convert stored glycogen back into glucose when blood sugar is low. Short sight (myopia) occurs when the eyeball is too long or the lens focuses light in front of (rather than directly on) the retina, making distant objects appear blurred; it is corrected using concave (diverging) lenses, which spread the light out slightly before it enters the eye so it focuses correctly on the retina. Deafness can be prevented by avoiding prolonged exposure to loud noise, promptly treating ear infections, avoiding inserting objects into the ear canal, and vaccinating against diseases (such as rubella and meningitis) known to cause hearing loss.

ECZ Biology Paper 2 (5090/2) Section B Essay, 2021, Question 12: (a) Explain the ecological terms habitat, community, and niche. (b) Using named examples, explain what a food chain is, and how energy flows through it. Model answer: A habitat is the specific place where an organism lives; a community is all the different populations of organisms living and interacting in a particular area; and a niche is the specific role an organism plays within its habitat and community, including how and what it feeds on. A food chain (for example, grass to grasshopper to frog to snake) shows the transfer of energy and nutrients from one organism to the next through feeding relationships, starting with a producer that captures energy from sunlight. Energy flows through the food chain in one direction, decreasing at each successive stage (trophic level) because much of the energy taken in at each level is lost as heat through respiration or is not consumed by the next level, meaning less energy is available to organisms further along the chain.

Introduction
Atomic structure is a topic consisting of six subtopics namely structure of an atom, properties of sub
atomic particles, atomic number and mass number, chemical symbols and nuclide notation, electronic
configuration. Atomic structure is the foundation of all forms of matter. Atoms are the building
blocks that compose everything we see, touch, and interact with in daily life, from the air we breathe

chemical reactions each form of matter can undergo and influences how substances behave and
interact. For example, understanding atomic structure enables advancements in energy generation

Key Learning Points:
An atom as the smallest particle of an element that retains its chemical properties and participates in
chemical reactions.
John Dalton, in the early 1800, suggested that atoms are indivisible and indestructible particles. Each
element is composed of identical atoms that have specific mass and properties.
J.J Thomson, in the late 1800, discovered the electron and suggested that atoms are composed of a
positively charged sphere with negatively charged electrons embedded within it, like plums in a
pudding.
Ernest Rutherford in 1911, based on gold foil experiments suggested that an atom consists of a small
positively charged dense nucleus at the center surrounded by electrons in a mostly empty space.
Niels Bohr, in 1913, building on Rutherford's, proposed a quantized model of atomic structure. The
model describes the arrangement of electrons around the nucleus in discrete energy levels, also
referred to as shells. According to Bohr’s model, electrons revolve around the nucleus in fixed circular
paths called "orbits" or "energy levels." Each orbit corresponds to a specific energy level, and
electrons can only occupy these discrete levels, not intermediate spaces. Electrons can move between
energy levels by absorbing or emitting energy in the form of photons. The energy change corresponds
to the difference between the two levels. Protons and neutrons are found in the nucleus at the centre
of an atom.

Sub–Topic 5: Electronic Configuration
Introduction
Electronic configuration refers to the distribution of electrons in the various energy levels or shells
surrounding an atom's nucleus. Understanding how electrons are arranged in these shells is essential
for grasping how atoms interact chemically and how they form bonds. The arrangement follows
specific rules, where electrons occupy the lowest available energy levels first based on the maximum
capacity of each shell. This foundational concept serves as an initiator to more advanced topics
in Chemistry and atomic theory.
Specific Competence:
 Apply electronic configuration of atoms
Key Terms:
 Electron (electronic) Configuration: Arrangement of electrons in the energy levels (shells) of
atoms
 Valence electrons: Electrons in the outermost shell of an atom
 Valency: The combining power of an element
 Duplet state: The state of stability of having two electrons in the outermost shell
 Octet state: The state of stability of having eight electrons in the outermost shell

Sub-Topic 6: Isotopic Nature of Elements
Introduction
Isotopes are variations of atoms that share the same number of protons but have different mass
numbers due to varying numbers of neutrons. Understanding isotopes is crucial in fields such
as Chemistry, Physics, and medicine, as they can affect the properties and behaviour of elements.
Specific Competences:
 Determine relative atomic mass of an element from relative isotopic masses
 Manage radioisotopes
Key Terms:
 Isotopes: Atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers
 Mass spectrum: A graph showing the distribution of isotopes of an element and their relative
abundances
 Mutagens: Agents that cause mutations in DNA, which can lead to cancer and other health
issues
 Nuclear energy: Energy produced through nuclear reactions involving isotopes
 Nuclear reactions: Reactions involving the particles in the nucleus
 Percentage abundance: The proportion of each isotope in a sample, expressed as a percentage

 Relative atomic mass: Average mass of the isotopes of an element based on their natural

Key Learning Points:
Radioactive isotopes have a wide range of applications which include the following:
 Medical treatment: Radioactive isotopes like Cobalt-60 are used in radiation therapy to target
and kill cancer cells while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissue.
 Isotopes such as Technetium-99 are used in imaging to diagnose conditions by highlighting
specific organs or tissues. Iodine -131 is used in the treatment of thyroid cancer
Hook: How do radioactive isotopes contribute to advancements in medicine, industry,

 Industrial uses: Isotopes are utilized in industries to trace the flow of substances. For instance,
radioactive tracers can be used in oil exploration to monitor the movement of fluids. They can
also be used to measure the thickness of materials in manufacturing processes.
 Archaeology: Carbon Dating: Carbon-14 dating is used to determine the age of ancient
artifacts and fossils by measuring the remaining carbon-14.
 Nuclear energy production: Isotopes like Uranium-235 and Plutonium-239 are used as fuel in
nuclear reactors to generate electricity.

Key Learning Points:
While radioactive isotopes are very useful, they also pose significant hazards which include the
following:
 Health risks: Exposure to radiation can lead to health issues, including cancer, radiation
sickness, and genetic mutations
 Environmental contamination: Radioactive waste can contaminate soil, water, and air,
leading to long-term ecological damage
 Nuclear accidents: Accidents at nuclear power plants such as Chernobyl and Fukushima can
release large amounts of radioactive materials, leading to widespread contamination and health
crises
 Mutagens: Radioactive isotopes can cause mutations in Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), leading
to potential genetic disorders in living organisms

Key Learning Points:
To mitigate the risks associated with radioactive isotopes, the following safe storage and handling
practices are essential:
 Shielding: Use appropriate shielding (lead, concrete) to protect against radiation exposure
 Containment: Store radioactive materials in secure, labelled containers to prevent leaks and
spills
 Access control: Limit access to areas where radioactive materials are stored to trained
personnel only
 Monitoring: Monitor levels of radiation in storage areas and during transportation using
radiation detectors
 Disposal: Follow regulations for the safe disposal of radioactive waste, ensuring it is
transported to designated facilities
 Training: Provide proper training for personnel handling radioactive substances to ensure they
adhere to safety protocols and emergency procedures
 Avoid pointing/directing radioactive sources towards people during use

The Agricultural Science Form 1 Teaching Module has been developed to guide teachers on how to
facilitate Competence-based lessons at this level. The module aims to provide quality education that is
aligned with the Competence-Based Curriculum and 21st Century Skills, that is developing knowledge,
skills, values and positive attitudes that are aimed at enabling Learners to live and grow into productive
and useful members of their communities and the Zambian society.
Agricultural Science will be taught using learner centred activities which will help learners to
acquire desirable lifelong skills, values and positive attitudes needed for their personal and national
development. Agricultural Science, as an applied science, will be taught in a practical manner, in
which learners will be involved in carrying out agricultural activities which will include growing crops,
rearing poultry, keeping livestock, apiculture and aquaculture.
Therefore, Agricultural Science activities will be done both in the laboratory where experiments will be
conducted and, in the field where field trials/observations will be done.
The assessment in the teaching module is part of the School-Based Assessment. It will be emphasised
by using various methods of testing according to sub-topics as part of the normal teaching and learning
process and therefore, the assessment opportunities will also occur during this normal process.
The assessment to measure each Specific Competence will occur in three forms:
 Observation – watching learners working (good for assessing skills and values)
 Conversation – asking questions and talking to learners (good for assessing knowledge and
understanding)
 Product – appraising the learners’ work (writing reports, translation, calculations, presentations,
maps, diagrams, models etc.) in this context the product is seen as something physical and
permanent that the teacher can keep and look at.

This module provides suggested learning activities and teaching strategies that enhance learners’
scientific competences. The suggested activities are designed to offer learners hands-on experiences
through manipulation of real objects, interaction with nature and learning through practice, bearing in
mind that the desired competences and 21st century skills will NOT be achieved by the topics ‘covered’
but by the manner in which teaching and learning activities are carried out.
Activities in the Module are meant to be incorporated in the teacher’s lesson plan. The activities are
NOT meant to replace the teacher’s lesson plan.
The topics and activities in the Module are as specified in the 2024 Agricultural Science Ordinary Level
Syllabus. There might be terms used in the Module that are not the same as those in the syllabus, but
this is in the interest of using language that might be more appropriate for the learner. Teachers are
encouraged to be mindful as well about what language is within the grasp of their learners and not use
terms simply because they appear in the syllabus.
Teachers are encouraged to start topics with a hook that provokes their learners’ curiosity and motivation.
A Hook is an opening statement at the start of the topic that seeks to stimulate learners’ curiosity
and motivate them to learn. A hook can be a provocative question, a scenario or something that puts
learners into a thinking mood. Let the designed hook incline towards problem-solving scenarios.
The purpose of the module is to illustrate teaching and learning activities that:
i. are consistent with teaching and learning in a competence-based curriculum,
ii. show processes that allow achievement of 21st Century Skills such as creativity, collaboration,
communication and critical thinking,
iii. illustrate what activity-based and learner-centred teaching and learning could look like.

TOPIC 3: CLIMATE-SMART AGRICUTURE
Introduction
Climate-Smart Agriculture is an approach to agriculture that aims to increase agricultural productivity
and improve food security while reducing damage to the environment. This topic focuses on climate-
smart agriculture and its main features. Introducing learners to Climate-Smart Agriculture will give them
an opportunity to think critically, observe and understand the common practices in the local area and
evaluate the positive and negative effects of the practices.
Hook: “How can Climate-Smart Agriculture ensure food security?”
General Competences
 Analytical thinking,
 Communication,
 Creativity and innovation,
 Collaboration,
 Critical thinking,
 Environmental sustainability and
 Problem solving.
Key Concepts/ Processes/ Terms
Drones - are unmanned aerial vehicles that may be equipped with sensors, cameras and other
technologies.
Productivity - the ability of agricultural systems to produce high quality crops and livestock per unit
input.
Adaptation - the process of adjusting agricultural practices, technologies and policies to cope with
the impacts of climate change.
Mitigation - the actions taken to minimize/ reduce the damage to the environment.
Sub-Topic 31- Climate-Smart Agriculture
Introduction:
Climate-Smart Agriculture is an approach to agriculture that aims to increase agricultural productivity,
improve food security and minimize greenhouse gas emissions. Learners will investigate key

and problem-solving skills.
Learners, after studying the subtopic, will develop positive attitudes towards the climate and use the
acquired skills in more sustainable way of growing crops and raising livestock.

The following are some of the activities you can use to teach the learners about growing a variety of
vegetables.

511 Exploring the common vegetables grown in the local environment according to their
groups, varieties and discuss their value.
Learning Materials: - Charts showing variety of crops and their values, Visual aids of specific vegetables,
Worksheet.
Activity 1: Exploring the common vegetables grown in the local environment.
Procedure
i. Put learners into small groups of 4 – 5.
ii. Ask learners to visit a farm, school garden or vegetable market to identify different types of
vegetables commonly grown locally.
iii. Let them make presentations to the class.
Supporting Activities
i. Participating in field days and agricultural shows.
ii. Consult electronic media.
Activity 2: Classify common vegetables grown in the local environment according to their
groups and varieties.
Procedure
i. Put learners into small groups of 4 – 5
ii. Using the charts of classes of vegetables, ask learners to classify the vegetables identified in activity
2 above.
iii. Ask learners to draw and display charts showing classification of vegetables to the class.
Supporting Activities
i. Visiting local farmers
ii. Viewing videos
iii. Consulting printing and electronic media
Activity 3: Discussing the nutritive value of common vegetables grown in the local
environment.
Procedure
i. Put learners into small groups of 4 – 5
ii. Ask learners to list vegetables in the activity 2 above.
iii. Allow learners to discuss the value (importance) of each of the vegetables in the list.
iv. Ask learners to make presentations to the class.
Supporting Activities
i. Consulting print media

ii. Viewing video clips
iii. Invite a nutritionist for a talk on value of various vegetables in human diet.
5.1.2 Selecting a site suitable for vegetable production
Activity 1: Selecting a site suitable for vegetable production
Procedure
i. In their previous groups from activity 2, ask learners to suggest characteristics of a good site for
vegetable growing in the school garden.
ii. Ask learners visit the school garden and demarcate portion where they are going to grow
vegetables.
iii. Ask learners to explain to the class how they arrived at the choices they made.
iv. Ask learners to record the activity of the day.
Supporting Activities
i. Invite extension and or agriculture expert for a talk on selection of vegetables.
ii. Visit a nearby vegetable farmer in the local area.
5.1.3 Designing a simple crop rotation plan including a legume vegetable (solanaceous -
legume – miscellaneous – leafy).
Learning Materials: ropes, pegs, hoes, slashers.
Activity 1: Designing a simple crop rotation plan including a legume vegetable
(solanaceous, - legume – miscellaneous – leafy).
Procedure
i. Put learners in groups of 4 -5.
ii. Ask learners to design models of a vegetable crop rotation.
iii. Ask learners to present and explain their designs.
iv. iSelect the best design and implement it in the field.
v. Ask learners to note the best design in their dairies.
Supporting Activities
i. Video clip of designing simple crop rotation.
ii. A talk/visit on a local vegetable growing farmer.
Tip:
 Crops of the same family should not follow each other.
 Deep rotted vegetables should alternate with shallow rooted ones.

 Heavy feeders should follow light feeders.
 A legume must be part of the rotation.
 Consider inclusion of fallow in the rotation.
5.1.4 Choosing one representative vegetable from leafy, solanaceous, legume and
miscellaneous for practicing.
Learning Materials: Work cards of vegetables
Activity 1: Choosing one representative vegetable from leafy, solanaceous, legume and
miscellaneous for practising.
Procedure
i. Adopt learner grouping for activity 5.1.3.
ii. Ask learners to select a crop based on the crop rotation plan.
iii. Ask learners to note the crops selected in their dairies.
Supporting Activities
A talk/visit on a local vegetable growing farmer.
Skills Developed
i. Collaborating – Learners working in groups, discussing importance and value of vegetables and
presenting crop rotation plan
ii. Communicating- presenting, draw charts classification of vegetables, Crop rotation
iii. Critically Analysing- Identifying, classifying, value of vegetables, suggestion of a good site
iv. Creating Innovating – designing a simple crop rotation plan, picking vegetables based on a crop
rotation plan
EXPECTED STANDARD
A variety of vegetables grown accordingly.
Assessment
Ask learners to carry out the following tasks:
Task 1: Attempt essay type questions.
i. Describe how the following factors affect site selection in vegetable production:
a. Climate
b. Soil type
c. Market access
d. Topography
ii. Explain why loam soil is most suitable for vegetable production.
